12  Portfolio Weighting and Rebalancing

Note

In this chapter, we systematically compare portfolio weighting schemes (e.g., value-weighted, equal-weighted, and several risk-based alternativesin the Vietnamese equity market. We quantify the impact of rebalancing frequency and transaction costs on realized performance, and develop practical tools for constructing implementable portfolios under the frictions characteristic of an emerging market.

Every portfolio construction decision ultimately reduces to two choices: which assets to hold, and how much to allocate to each. While earlier chapters have focused on the first question, using factor models, anomalies, and fundamental analysis to select stocks, this chapter addresses the second. The weighting scheme a researcher or investor applies can fundamentally alter the conclusions drawn from portfolio-level tests and the returns earned from an investment strategy.

The distinction matters more in Vietnam than in large, liquid markets. The Vietnamese equity market features extreme skewness in the market capitalization distribution: the top 10 firms on HOSE account for roughly 50% of total market capitalization, while hundreds of small firms contribute negligible weight. Under value-weighting, a portfolio’s performance is dominated by a handful of large-cap names (Vinhomes, Vingroup, Vietcombank, FPT). Under equal-weighting, every firm contributes equally, tilting the portfolio toward small, illiquid stocks that may be expensive or impossible to trade at scale. Neither scheme is inherently correct; the choice depends on the question being asked.

This chapter develops the analytical framework for making that choice. We begin with the theoretical properties of weighting schemes, implement each scheme in practice with Vietnamese data, quantify the transaction costs of rebalancing, and extend the analysis to risk-based alternatives that explicitly incorporate the covariance structure of returns.

12.1 Theoretical Framework

12.1.1 Value-Weighted Portfolios

A value-weighted (VW) portfolio allocates to each stock in proportion to its market capitalization:

\[ w_{i,t}^{VW} = \frac{\text{MCap}_{i,t}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_t} \text{MCap}_{j,t}} \tag{12.1}\]

where \(\text{MCap}_{i,t} = P_{i,t} \times \text{Shares}_{i,t}\) is the market capitalization of stock \(i\) at time \(t\).

The VW portfolio has a unique theoretical status: it is the portfolio that all investors collectively hold (the “market portfolio” in CAPM). Its key properties are:

  1. Self-rebalancing. As prices move, weights adjust automatically. A VW portfolio requires trading only when constituents enter or leave the index, or when corporate actions (splits, issuances) change shares outstanding.
  2. Low turnover. Because weights drift with prices rather than being reset to targets, VW portfolios have minimal rebalancing costs.
  3. Large-cap bias. Returns are dominated by the largest firms. In Vietnam, this means the portfolio’s risk-return profile is heavily influenced by banking, real estate, and technology conglomerates.

Hsu (2006) argues that VW portfolios are sub-optimal because they mechanically overweight overpriced stocks and underweight underpriced stocks (i.e., any deviation of price from fundamental value creates a systematic drag on VW performance relative to a fundamentally weighted alternative).

12.1.2 Equal-Weighted Portfolios

An equal-weighted (EW) portfolio assigns the same weight to each constituent:

\[ w_{i,t}^{EW} = \frac{1}{N_t} \tag{12.2}\]

DeMiguel, Garlappi, and Uppal (2009) show that the 1/N portfolio is surprisingly competitive with mean-variance optimized portfolios, particularly when estimation windows are short and the number of assets is large (conditions that closely describe the Vietnamese market). The intuition is that estimation error in expected returns and covariances can overwhelm the gains from optimization, making the “naive” equal-weight scheme a robust default.

Plyakha, Uppal, and Vilkov (2021) decompose the EW outperformance over VW into two components:

  1. Size tilt. EW allocates more to small firms, which historically earn a size premium.
  2. Rebalancing bonus. Monthly rebalancing back to equal weights is a contrarian strategy: it sells recent winners and buys recent losers, profiting from mean reversion in individual stock returns.

However, the EW portfolio has practical disadvantages that are particularly severe in Vietnam:

  • High turnover. Every rebalancing date requires trading every stock back to equal weight.
  • Illiquidity exposure. Equal weighting of micro-cap stocks that trade VND 100 million/day alongside large-caps trading VND 500 billion/day creates severe implementation challenges.
  • Price impact. In a market with daily price limits (\(\pm\) 7% on HOSE, \(\pm\) 10% on HNX), rebalancing trades for illiquid names may hit limit-up or limit-down, preventing full execution.

12.2.1 Universe Construction and Liquidity Filters

A critical pre-processing step is defining the investable universe. Including all listed stocks, regardless of liquidity, inflates the apparent benefits of equal-weighting and other small-cap-tilted schemes because it implicitly assumes the ability to trade illiquid stocks without friction. We apply graduated liquidity filters and track how results change.

def construct_universe(monthly_df, min_mcap_pct=0, min_turnover=0, min_months=12):
    """
    Construct investable universe with liquidity filters.

    Parameters
    ----------
    min_mcap_pct : float
        Exclude stocks below this market cap percentile (0-100).
    min_turnover : float
        Minimum average daily turnover in VND billion.
    min_months : int
        Minimum months of return history required.
    """
    df = monthly_df.copy()

    # Market cap percentile filter (within each month)
    if min_mcap_pct > 0:
        df["mcap_pctile"] = df.groupby("month_end")["market_cap"].transform(
            lambda x: x.rank(pct=True) * 100
        )
        df = df[df["mcap_pctile"] >= min_mcap_pct]

    # Turnover filter
    if min_turnover > 0:
        df = df[df["turnover_value_avg_20d"] >= min_turnover * 1e9]

    # History filter
    ticker_months = df.groupby("ticker")["month_end"].transform("count")
    df = df[ticker_months >= min_months]

    return df

12.3 Implementing Weighting Schemes

We now implement each weighting scheme and compute monthly portfolio returns. All implementations follow a common structure: at each rebalancing date, compute target weights from available information, then compute the weighted return over the subsequent holding period.

12.3.1 Core Portfolio Engine

def compute_portfolio_returns(
    monthly_df,
    weight_fn,
    rebal_freq="M",
    max_weight=1.0,
    min_weight=0.0,
):
    """
    Compute time series of portfolio returns for a given weighting function.

    Parameters
    ----------
    monthly_df : DataFrame
        Must contain 'ticker', 'month_end', 'monthly_return', and any
        columns needed by weight_fn.
    weight_fn : callable
        Function that takes a cross-section DataFrame and returns a
        Series of weights indexed by ticker. Weights need not sum to 1
        (they will be normalized).
    rebal_freq : str
        'M' for monthly, 'Q' for quarterly, 'A' for annual.
    max_weight : float
        Maximum weight per stock (for capped schemes).
    min_weight : float
        Minimum weight per stock.

    Returns
    -------
    DataFrame with columns: month_end, port_return, n_stocks,
    turnover, hhi, effective_n
    """
    months = sorted(monthly_df["month_end"].unique())

    # Determine rebalancing dates
    if rebal_freq == "M":
        rebal_dates = set(months)
    elif rebal_freq == "Q":
        rebal_dates = set(pd.to_datetime(months).to_period("Q").to_timestamp("M"))
        # Map to nearest month-end
        rebal_dates = {m for m in months if pd.Timestamp(m).month % 3 == 0}
        if not rebal_dates:
            rebal_dates = set(months[::3])
    elif rebal_freq == "A":
        rebal_dates = {m for m in months if pd.Timestamp(m).month == 6}
        if not rebal_dates:
            rebal_dates = set(months[::12])
    else:
        rebal_dates = set(months)

    results = []
    prev_weights = None

    for month in months:
        cross_section = monthly_df[monthly_df["month_end"] == month].copy()
        cross_section = cross_section.dropna(subset=["monthly_return"])

        if len(cross_section) < 5:
            continue

        if month in rebal_dates or prev_weights is None:
            # Compute fresh weights
            raw_weights = weight_fn(cross_section)
            raw_weights = raw_weights.clip(lower=min_weight, upper=max_weight)
            total = raw_weights.sum()
            if total <= 0:
                continue
            target_weights = raw_weights / total
        else:
            # Drift weights forward from previous month
            if prev_weights is not None:
                available = cross_section.set_index("ticker")
                drifted = prev_weights.reindex(available.index, fill_value=0)
                # Adjust for returns
                drifted = drifted * (1 + available["monthly_return"])
                total = drifted.sum()
                if total <= 0:
                    continue
                target_weights = drifted / total
            else:
                continue

        # Align weights with available stocks
        cross_section = cross_section.set_index("ticker")
        aligned_w = target_weights.reindex(cross_section.index, fill_value=0)
        aligned_w = aligned_w / aligned_w.sum()

        # Portfolio return
        port_ret = (aligned_w * cross_section["monthly_return"]).sum()

        # Turnover (two-way)
        if prev_weights is not None:
            prev_aligned = prev_weights.reindex(aligned_w.index, fill_value=0)
            # Drift previous weights
            prev_drifted = prev_aligned * (
                1
                + cross_section["monthly_return"].reindex(
                    prev_aligned.index, fill_value=0
                )
            )
            prev_drifted = (
                prev_drifted / prev_drifted.sum()
                if prev_drifted.sum() > 0
                else prev_drifted
            )
            turnover = (aligned_w - prev_drifted).abs().sum() / 2
        else:
            turnover = 1.0

        # Concentration metrics
        hhi = (aligned_w**2).sum()
        effective_n = 1.0 / hhi if hhi > 0 else 0

        results.append(
            {
                "month_end": month,
                "port_return": port_ret,
                "n_stocks": (aligned_w > 1e-6).sum(),
                "turnover": turnover,
                "hhi": hhi,
                "effective_n": effective_n,
            }
        )

        prev_weights = aligned_w.copy()

    return pd.DataFrame(results)

12.3.4 Capped Value-Weighted Portfolio

To mitigate the concentration of pure VW while retaining its low-turnover properties, we impose a cap on individual stock weights. A common choice is 5% or 10%, mimicking the construction rules of capped indices such as the MSCI Capped indices.

def capped_vw_weights(cross_section, cap=0.05):
    """Capped VW: market cap weights with an upper bound."""
    w = cross_section.set_index('ticker')['market_cap']
    w = w / w.sum()
    # Iterative capping (redistribute excess weight)
    for _ in range(20):
        excess = w[w > cap] - cap
        if excess.sum() <= 1e-8:
            break
        w[w > cap] = cap
        w[w <= cap] *= (1 + excess.sum() / w[w <= cap].sum())
    return w

capped5 = compute_portfolio_returns(
    universe_mid, lambda cs: capped_vw_weights(cs, 0.05), rebal_freq='M'
)
capped10 = compute_portfolio_returns(
    universe_mid, lambda cs: capped_vw_weights(cs, 0.10), rebal_freq='M'
)

print(f"Capped 5%: eff_N = {capped5['effective_n'].mean():.1f}, "
      f"turnover = {capped5['turnover'].mean():.4f}")
print(f"Capped 10%: eff_N = {capped10['effective_n'].mean():.1f}, "
      f"turnover = {capped10['turnover'].mean():.4f}")

12.3.5 Fundamental-Weighted Portfolio

Arnott, Hsu, and Moore (2005) propose weighting stocks by fundamental measures (revenue, book equity, dividends, cash flow) rather than market cap. The logic is that fundamental weights are not contaminated by pricing errors, breaking the mechanical overweighting of overvalued stocks inherent in VW. We construct a composite fundamental weight using the average rank across four measures:

\[ w_{i,t}^{FW} \propto \frac{1}{4}\left(\text{Rank}_{i,t}^{\text{Rev}} + \text{Rank}_{i,t}^{\text{BE}} + \text{Rank}_{i,t}^{\text{Div}} + \text{Rank}_{i,t}^{\text{CFO}}\right) \tag{12.3}\]

# Merge fundamentals with monthly data (use most recent fiscal year)
fundamentals["merge_year"] = fundamentals["fiscal_year"] + 1  # Lag by 1 year
monthly_fund = monthly.copy()
monthly_fund["year"] = pd.to_datetime(monthly_fund["month_end"]).dt.year

monthly_fund = monthly_fund.merge(
    fundamentals.rename(columns={"merge_year": "year"}),
    on=["ticker", "year"],
    how="left",
)


def fw_weights(cross_section):
    """Fundamental-weighted: composite of revenue, book equity, dividends, CFO."""
    cs = cross_section.set_index("ticker")
    ranks = pd.DataFrame(index=cs.index)

    for col in ["revenue", "book_equity", "dividends_paid", "operating_cash_flow"]:
        if col in cs.columns:
            vals = cs[col].clip(lower=0)  # Only positive values
            ranks[col] = vals.rank(pct=True)

    composite = ranks.mean(axis=1)
    composite = composite.fillna(0)
    return composite

12.4 Risk-Based Weighting Schemes

The weighting schemes above use only market cap or accounting data. Risk-based schemes incorporate the covariance structure of returns, aiming to produce portfolios with better risk-adjusted performance. The trade-off is that they require estimating the covariance matrix (i.e., a high-dimensional object that is notoriously difficult to estimate precisely with short time series).

12.4.1 Covariance Estimation

With \(N \approx 300\) stocks and \(T \approx 60\) months, the sample covariance matrix is severely ill-conditioned. We use the Ledoit and Wolf (2004) shrinkage estimator, which pulls the sample covariance toward a structured target (the identity matrix scaled by the average variance):

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\text{shrink}} = \delta \mathbf{F} + (1 - \delta) \mathbf{S} \tag{12.4}\]

where \(\mathbf{S}\) is the sample covariance, \(\mathbf{F}\) is the shrinkage target, and \(\delta \in [0,1]\) is the optimal shrinkage intensity derived analytically.

def estimate_covariance(monthly_df, month, lookback=60, min_obs=36):
    """
    Estimate covariance matrix using Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage.
    
    Parameters
    ----------
    monthly_df : DataFrame with ticker, month_end, monthly_return
    month : target month (use returns before this date)
    lookback : number of months to use
    min_obs : minimum observations per stock
    
    Returns
    -------
    cov_matrix : DataFrame (N x N)
    tickers : list of tickers with sufficient data
    """
    end_date = pd.Timestamp(month)
    start_date = end_date - pd.DateOffset(months=lookback)
    
    window = monthly_df[
        (monthly_df['month_end'] > start_date) &
        (monthly_df['month_end'] <= end_date)
    ]
    
    # Pivot to wide format
    returns_wide = window.pivot_table(
        index='month_end', columns='ticker', values='monthly_return'
    )
    
    # Keep stocks with sufficient observations
    valid_cols = returns_wide.columns[returns_wide.notna().sum() >= min_obs]
    returns_wide = returns_wide[valid_cols].dropna(axis=0, how='all')
    
    # Fill remaining NAs with 0 (conservative)
    returns_clean = returns_wide.fillna(0)
    
    if returns_clean.shape[1] < 10:
        return None, None
    
    # Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage
    lw = LedoitWolf()
    lw.fit(returns_clean.values)
    
    cov_matrix = pd.DataFrame(
        lw.covariance_,
        index=valid_cols, columns=valid_cols
    )
    
    return cov_matrix, list(valid_cols)

12.4.2 Minimum Variance Portfolio

The global minimum variance (GMV) portfolio minimizes portfolio variance without targeting a specific return level (Clarke, De Silva, and Thorley 2011):

\[ \mathbf{w}^{MV} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}} \; \mathbf{w}'\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\mathbf{w} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{1}'\mathbf{w} = 1, \; w_i \geq 0 \tag{12.5}\]

The long-only constraint (\(w_i \geq 0\)) is essential in practice and also acts as an implicit shrinkage that improves out-of-sample performance (Jagannathan and Ma 2003).

def minimum_variance_weights(cov_matrix, max_weight=0.05):
    """
    Solve for the minimum variance portfolio with long-only
    and position-size constraints.
    """
    n = cov_matrix.shape[0]
    Sigma = cov_matrix.values
    
    def portfolio_variance(w):
        return w @ Sigma @ w
    
    constraints = [
        {'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda w: np.sum(w) - 1}
    ]
    bounds = [(0, max_weight) for _ in range(n)]
    x0 = np.ones(n) / n
    
    result = minimize(
        portfolio_variance, x0,
        method='SLSQP',
        bounds=bounds,
        constraints=constraints,
        options={'maxiter': 1000, 'ftol': 1e-12}
    )
    
    if result.success:
        return pd.Series(result.x, index=cov_matrix.index)
    else:
        return pd.Series(1.0 / n, index=cov_matrix.index)

def mv_weight_fn(cross_section, cov_cache={}):
    """Wrapper for portfolio engine: minimum variance."""
    month = cross_section['month_end'].iloc[0]
    
    if month not in cov_cache:
        cov_matrix, tickers = estimate_covariance(monthly, month)
        cov_cache[month] = (cov_matrix, tickers)
    
    cov_matrix, tickers = cov_cache[month]
    if cov_matrix is None:
        # Fallback to equal weight
        return pd.Series(1.0, index=cross_section.set_index('ticker').index)
    
    # Restrict to stocks in both cross-section and covariance matrix
    available = set(cross_section['ticker']) & set(tickers)
    if len(available) < 10:
        return pd.Series(1.0, index=cross_section.set_index('ticker').index)
    
    sub_cov = cov_matrix.loc[list(available), list(available)]
    weights = minimum_variance_weights(sub_cov)
    
    return weights

12.4.3 Risk Parity (Equal Risk Contribution)

Risk parity allocates so that each asset contributes equally to total portfolio risk (Maillard, Roncalli, and Teı̈letche 2010). The risk contribution of asset \(i\) is:

\[ RC_i = w_i \cdot \frac{(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w})_i}{\sqrt{\mathbf{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}}} \tag{12.6}\]

The risk parity portfolio solves \(RC_i = RC_j\) for all \(i, j\):

def risk_parity_weights(cov_matrix, max_weight=0.05):
    """
    Solve for the risk parity portfolio where each asset
    contributes equally to total portfolio variance.
    """
    n = cov_matrix.shape[0]
    Sigma = cov_matrix.values
    
    def risk_parity_objective(w):
        port_var = w @ Sigma @ w
        marginal = Sigma @ w
        risk_contrib = w * marginal
        target_rc = port_var / n
        return np.sum((risk_contrib - target_rc) ** 2)
    
    constraints = [
        {'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda w: np.sum(w) - 1}
    ]
    bounds = [(1e-6, max_weight) for _ in range(n)]
    x0 = np.ones(n) / n
    
    result = minimize(
        risk_parity_objective, x0,
        method='SLSQP',
        bounds=bounds,
        constraints=constraints,
        options={'maxiter': 1000, 'ftol': 1e-12}
    )
    
    if result.success:
        return pd.Series(result.x, index=cov_matrix.index)
    else:
        return pd.Series(1.0 / n, index=cov_matrix.index)

def rp_weight_fn(cross_section, cov_cache={}):

12.4.4 Maximum Diversification Portfolio

Choueifaty (2008) define the diversification ratio as the ratio of weighted average volatility to portfolio volatility:

\[ DR(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}'\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}}} \tag{12.7}\]

where \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) is the vector of individual asset volatilities. The maximum diversification portfolio maximizes this ratio:

def max_diversification_weights(cov_matrix, max_weight=0.05):
    """Maximize the diversification ratio."""
    n = cov_matrix.shape[0]
    Sigma = cov_matrix.values
    sigma = np.sqrt(np.diag(Sigma))
    
    def neg_div_ratio(w):
        port_vol = np.sqrt(w @ Sigma @ w)
        if port_vol < 1e-10:
            return 0
        return -(w @ sigma) / port_vol
    
    constraints = [
        {'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda w: np.sum(w) - 1}
    ]
    bounds = [(0, max_weight) for _ in range(n)]
    x0 = np.ones(n) / n
    
    result = minimize(
        neg_div_ratio, x0,
        method='SLSQP',
        bounds=bounds,
        constraints=constraints,
        options={'maxiter': 1000, 'ftol': 1e-12}
    )
    
    if result.success:
        return pd.Series(result.x, index=cov_matrix.index)
    else:
        return pd.Series(1.0 / n, index=cov_matrix.index)

12.6.1 Estimating Trading Costs in Vietnam

Transaction costs in Vietnam include explicit components (brokerage commissions, exchange fees, taxes) and implicit components (bid-ask spread, price impact). The explicit cost structure as of 2024 is approximately:

The total explicit round-trip cost (buy + sell) ranges from approximately 0.30% to 0.80%. Implicit costs—the spread and price impact—can be substantially larger for small and illiquid stocks.

We model total transaction costs as a function of trade size and stock liquidity:

\[ TC_{i,t} = c_{\text{fixed}} + \frac{1}{2} \text{Spread}_{i,t} + \lambda \sqrt{\frac{|\Delta w_{i,t}| \cdot \text{AUM}}{ADV_{i,t}}} \tag{12.8}\]

where \(c_{\text{fixed}} \approx 0.25\%\) is the explicit cost per trade, \(\text{Spread}_{i,t}\) is the quoted bid-ask spread, \(\Delta w_{i,t}\) is the weight change, \(\text{AUM}\) is portfolio size, \(ADV_{i,t}\) is average daily volume in VND, and \(\lambda\) is the price impact coefficient estimated from the Amihud (2002) model.

def estimate_transaction_costs(weight_changes, stock_data,
                                aum_vnd=100e9, fixed_cost=0.0025,
                                impact_coef=0.10):
    """
    Estimate total transaction costs for a rebalancing event.
    
    Parameters
    ----------
    weight_changes : Series indexed by ticker, absolute weight changes
    stock_data : DataFrame with ticker, bid_ask_spread, turnover_value_avg_20d
    aum_vnd : float, portfolio AUM in VND
    fixed_cost : float, explicit cost per unit traded (one-way)
    impact_coef : float, price impact coefficient (lambda)
    
    Returns
    -------
    total_cost : float, total TC as fraction of AUM
    cost_detail : DataFrame with per-stock costs
    """
    costs = []
    
    for ticker, dw in weight_changes.items():
        if abs(dw) < 1e-6:
            continue
        
        trade_vnd = abs(dw) * aum_vnd
        
        # Explicit cost (one-way)
        explicit = fixed_cost * trade_vnd
        
        # Spread cost
        stock_info = stock_data[stock_data['ticker'] == ticker]
        if len(stock_info) > 0:
            spread = stock_info['bid_ask_spread'].iloc[0]
            adv = stock_info['turnover_value_avg_20d'].iloc[0]
        else:
            spread = 0.005  # Default 50 bps
            adv = 1e9  # Default VND 1bn
        
        spread_cost = 0.5 * spread * trade_vnd
        
        # Price impact (square root model)
        participation_rate = trade_vnd / max(adv, 1e6)
        impact_cost = impact_coef * np.sqrt(participation_rate) * trade_vnd
        
        total = explicit + spread_cost + impact_cost
        costs.append({
            'ticker': ticker,
            'weight_change': dw,
            'trade_vnd': trade_vnd,
            'explicit': explicit,
            'spread': spread_cost,
            'impact': impact_cost,
            'total': total
        })
    
    cost_df = pd.DataFrame(costs)
    total_cost = cost_df['total'].sum() / aum_vnd if len(cost_df) > 0 else 0
    
    return total_cost, cost_df

12.7 Rebalancing Frequency Analysis

12.7.1 The Rebalancing Trade-Off

Rebalancing serves two purposes: (i) restoring target weights to maintain the desired risk profile, and (ii) harvesting the “rebalancing bonus”—the systematic profit from buying low and selling high that arises when weights are reset to targets in the presence of mean-reverting cross-sectional returns.

The trade-off is clear: more frequent rebalancing maintains tighter adherence to target weights and captures more of the rebalancing bonus, but incurs higher transaction costs. The optimal frequency depends on the magnitude of mean reversion (which determines the gross rebalancing bonus), the level of transaction costs, and the rate at which weights drift from targets.

12.7.2 Threshold-Based Rebalancing

An alternative to calendar-based rebalancing is to rebalance only when portfolio weights drift beyond a tolerance band. This “no-trade zone” approach is motivated by Gârleanu and Pedersen (2013), who derive the optimal dynamic trading strategy under quadratic transaction costs and show that the optimal portfolio is a weighted average of the current holdings and the frictionless target.

def compute_threshold_rebalanced(monthly_df, weight_fn,
                                  threshold=0.01):
    """
    Rebalance only when maximum weight deviation exceeds threshold.
    
    Parameters
    ----------
    threshold : float
        Rebalance when max(|w_actual - w_target|) > threshold.
    """
    months = sorted(monthly_df['month_end'].unique())
    results = []
    current_weights = None
    rebalance_count = 0
    
    for month in months:
        cs = monthly_df[monthly_df['month_end'] == month].copy()
        cs = cs.dropna(subset=['monthly_return']).set_index('ticker')
        
        if len(cs) < 5:
            continue
        
        target = weight_fn(cs.reset_index())
        target = target / target.sum()
        
        if current_weights is None:
            current_weights = target.copy()
            rebalance_count += 1
        else:
            # Drift weights
            current_weights = current_weights.reindex(cs.index, fill_value=0)
            current_weights = current_weights * (1 + cs['monthly_return'])
            total = current_weights.sum()
            if total > 0:
                current_weights = current_weights / total
            
            # Check if rebalancing needed
            max_dev = (current_weights - target.reindex(
                current_weights.index, fill_value=0
            )).abs().max()
            
            if max_dev > threshold:
                turnover = (current_weights - target.reindex(
                    current_weights.index, fill_value=0
                )).abs().sum() / 2
                current_weights = target.reindex(cs.index, fill_value=0)
                current_weights = current_weights / current_weights.sum()
                rebalance_count += 1
            else:
                turnover = 0
        
        port_ret = (current_weights.reindex(cs.index, fill_value=0) *
                    cs['monthly_return']).sum()
        hhi = (current_weights ** 2).sum()
        
        results.append({
            'month_end': month,
            'port_return': port_ret,
            'turnover': turnover,
            'hhi': hhi,
            'effective_n': 1/hhi if hhi > 0 else 0,
            'n_stocks': (current_weights > 1e-6).sum()
        })
    
    print(f"Threshold {threshold:.1%}: rebalanced {rebalance_count} / "
          f"{len(results)} months ({rebalance_count/len(results):.0%})")
    return pd.DataFrame(results)

12.8 The Rebalancing Bonus: Decomposition

The excess return of the rebalanced EW portfolio over a buy-and-hold EW portfolio (which starts equal-weighted but drifts) can be decomposed following Plyakha, Uppal, and Vilkov (2021). Define \(r_i\) as the return of stock \(i\) over one period:

\[ R^{EW}_{\text{rebal}} - R^{EW}_{\text{drift}} \approx \frac{1}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^N \text{Var}(r_i) - \frac{1}{2N^2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\text{Cov}(r_i, r_j) \tag{12.9}\]

The first term captures the “buy low, sell high” effect from resetting weights after return dispersion. The second term is the cost of undoing covariance-induced drift. The rebalancing bonus is larger when cross-sectional return dispersion is high (which it is in Vietnam) and when pairwise correlations are low.

12.10 Machine Learning and the Implementable Efficient Frontier

Every scheme in this chapter was chosen first and charged for trading afterwards. Jensen et al. (2026) argue that this order is backwards for any strategy that trades on signals, and that the right object of evaluation is the implementable efficient frontier: the highest expected return net of trading costs attainable at each level of risk. They build a portfolio method around that objective, apply machine learning to it, and show that the ranking of strategies and of the characteristics that drive them changes once costs enter the objective rather than the accounting.

12.10.1 The Problem

An investor with wealth \(W_t\) holds portfolio weights \(\pi_t\) in \(N\) stocks. Between rebalancing dates the weights drift with returns, so the portfolio inherited at \(t\) is \(g_t \pi_{t-1}\), where \(g_t\) is a diagonal matrix with entries \((1 + r^f_t + r_{i,t})/(1 + g^W_t)\) and \(g^W_t\) is the growth of wealth. Moving to \(\pi_t\) requires trading \(W_t(\pi_t - g_t\pi_{t-1})\) in currency units, and trading moves prices linearly in the trade size, with a diagonal price-impact matrix \(\Lambda_t\). The return net of the risk-free rate and of trading costs is

\[ r^{\text{net}}_{t+1} = \pi_t^\top r_{t+1} - \frac{W_t}{2}\left(\pi_t - g_t\pi_{t-1}\right)^\top \Lambda_t \left(\pi_t - g_t\pi_{t-1}\right), \tag{12.11}\]

and the investor maximizes its mean minus \(\gamma/2\) times its variance. Two features of Equation 12.11 matter. Trading costs are quadratic in the trade, which is the linear-price-impact specification of Gârleanu and Pedersen (2013) rather than the square-root model in Equation 12.8; the choice buys tractability at the cost of understating the cost of small trades. And costs scale with wealth: the same weights cost a larger investor more. The authors calibrate impact so that trading 1% of a stock’s daily dollar volume moves its price by 0.1%, which gives \(\lambda_{i,t} = 0.2 / \text{ADV}_{i,t}\).

The frontier traced by varying \(\gamma\) has a property the textbook frontier lacks. Without costs, any point on the capital market line is reached by levering the tangency portfolio, so the Sharpe ratio is constant along the line. With costs, larger positions mean larger trades, so the net Sharpe ratio declines along the implementable frontier. An investor therefore cannot maximize the net Sharpe ratio and then choose leverage; risk, return, and cost have to be chosen together. And the frontier of a larger investor lies everywhere below that of a smaller one.

12.10.2 The Solution: Trade Toward an Aim

Generalizing Gârleanu and Pedersen (2013) to stationary returns and to expected returns that are arbitrary functions of characteristics, the authors show that the optimal portfolio takes the form

\[ \pi_t = m\, g_t \pi_{t-1} + (I - m)\, A_t , \tag{12.12}\]

so the investor starts from the inherited portfolio and trades a fraction \(I - m\) of the way toward an aim portfolio \(A_t\). The matrix \(m\) solves a fixed-point equation in \(\gamma\Sigma\) and \(W\Lambda\) and governs trading speed: it rises toward the identity, meaning slower trading, as wealth or price impact rise and as risk aversion falls. The aim is a discounted sum of current and expected future Markowitz portfolios,

\[ A_t \approx (I - m)^{-1} \sum_{\tau = 0}^{\infty} (m\bar{g})^{\tau}\, c\; \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\frac{1}{\gamma}\Sigma^{-1}\mu_{t+\tau}\right], \tag{12.13}\]

with \(c\) a matrix that depends on \(m\). Because the investor will hold whatever it buys today for a while, a signal that predicts returns for many months is worth more than an equally strong signal that decays within a month. This is the economic content of “alpha decay,” and it is exactly what a cost-agnostic or a static cost-aware portfolio ignores. The static alternative, which trades toward today’s Markowitz portfolio while penalizing today’s trade, has the same partial-adjustment form but aims at the wrong target.

12.10.3 Portfolio-ML: Learning the Aim Directly

Implementing Equation 12.13 requires forecasts of returns at every horizon. The authors’ preferred method, Portfolio-ML, skips return forecasting altogether and learns the aim directly. It parameterizes the aim as a linear function of random Fourier features of the characteristics, scaled by each stock’s volatility,

\[ A_t = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_t)^{-1}\, \text{RF}(s_t)\,\beta , \tag{12.14}\]

an idea that extends the parametric portfolio policies of Brandt, Santa-Clara, and Valkanov (2009) to a dynamic problem with trading costs. The random features, \(\cos(s_{i,t}^\top w_j)\) and \(\sin(s_{i,t}^\top w_j)\) for random Gaussian vectors \(w_j\), let a linear model approximate nonlinear functions and interactions of the characteristics, the device whose statistical properties in return prediction are studied by Kelly, Malamud, and Zhou (2024). Substituting Equation 12.14 into Equation 12.12 makes the portfolio approximately linear in \(\beta\), \(\pi_t \approx \Omega_t\beta\), where \(\Omega_t\) is a persistence- and growth-weighted average of current and past scaled signals. Realized utility is then quadratic in \(\beta\), and the optimal coefficients have a closed form:

\[ \hat{\beta} = \left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_t D_t + \ell I\right)^{-1} \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \Omega_t^\top r_{t+1}, \qquad D_t = \gamma\, \Omega_t^\top \Sigma_t \Omega_t + W\, \Delta\Omega_t^\top \Lambda_t\, \Delta\Omega_t , \tag{12.15}\]

where \(\Delta\Omega_t = \Omega_t - g_t\Omega_{t-1}\) is the implied trade and \(\ell\) is a ridge penalty. The number of random features, their scale, and \(\ell\) are chosen each year by an expanding-window validation on realized utility. No second tuning layer is needed, because the objective that fits \(\beta\) is already the investor’s objective.

The Markowitz portfolio built on machine-learning return forecasts has a gross Sharpe ratio of 2.0 but turnover so extreme that its net return is catastrophically negative. A standard decile long-short sort on the same forecasts turns over 260% of capital a month and has a net Sharpe ratio of about −12.
  • Static cost awareness helps but misplaces the investor. The static method earns a positive net Sharpe ratio of 0.94 yet a negative realized utility, because its realized risk far exceeds its ex ante risk.
  • Portfolio-ML dominates. It earns a net Sharpe ratio of 1.38 with a monthly turnover of 32%, and the highest realized utility. Even after the authors give the forecast-based methods a second tuning layer that rescales their inputs, Portfolio-ML beats them by roughly 20% in net Sharpe ratio and 60% in utility.
  • Costs change which characteristics matter. Measured by the utility lost when a characteristic is withheld, the “economic feature importance” of short-term reversal, which dominates cost-agnostic models, collapses, and value and quality become the most important characteristics.

12.10.5 An Independent Implementation

The core of the method is five small functions: the trading-speed matrix \(m\), the scaling of signals, random Fourier features, the implied-weights matrix \(\Omega_t\), and the closed-form coefficients of Equation 12.15.

def trading_speed(sigma, lam, wealth, gamma, mu=0.007, rf=0.0, iters=10):
    """
    Persistence matrix m in the optimal rule pi_t = m g_t pi_{t-1} + (I - m) A_t.

    Solves the fixed point m~ = (x + I + diag(G) - m~ o G)^{-1}, where
    x = (gamma / wealth) Lambda^{-1/2} Sigma Lambda^{-1/2}, G is the second
    moment of portfolio-weight growth, and m = Lambda^{-1/2} m~ Lambda^{1/2}.
    `lam` is the diagonal of the price-impact matrix (per unit of currency).
    """
    n = len(lam)
    G = np.ones((n, n)) + sigma / (1 + rf + mu) ** 2
    lam_isqrt = 1 / np.sqrt(lam)
    x = (gamma / wealth) * sigma * np.outer(lam_isqrt, lam_isqrt)
    # Start from the closed-form solution when weight growth is deterministic
    ev, evec = np.linalg.eigh(x + 2 * np.eye(n))
    m_tilde = evec @ np.diag(0.5 * (ev - np.sqrt(ev**2 - 4))) @ evec.T
    base = x + np.eye(n) + np.diag(np.diag(G))
    for _ in range(iters):
        m_tilde = np.linalg.inv(base - m_tilde * G)
    return lam_isqrt[:, None] * m_tilde * np.sqrt(lam)[None, :]


def scale_signals(features, vol):
    """Demean features, add a constant, give each column unit length, divide rows by volatility."""
    f = features - features.mean(axis=0)
    f = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(f)), f])
    f = f / np.sqrt((f**2).sum(axis=0))
    return f / vol[:, None]


def random_fourier_features(features, W):
    """Random Fourier features: cos and sin of random projections of the features."""
    z = features @ W
    return np.hstack([np.cos(z), np.sin(z)])


def implied_weights(S_hist, g_hist, m):
    """
    Omega_t such that the portfolio implied by aim coefficients beta is Omega_t beta.
    S_hist[i] is the scaled signal matrix i months ago (i = 0 is today);
    g_hist[i] is the weight-growth vector realized over the month ending i months ago.
    """
    n = m.shape[0]
    M, num, den = np.eye(n), np.zeros_like(S_hist[0]), np.zeros((n, n))
    for i, S_i in enumerate(S_hist):
        num += M @ S_i
        den += M
        if i < len(g_hist):
            M = M @ m @ np.diag(g_hist[i])
    return np.linalg.solve(den, num)


def portfolio_ml_moments(S, g, ret, sigma, lam, m, wealth, gamma, months, lookback=11):
    """Per-month ingredients of the Portfolio-ML objective: r_tilde_t and D_t."""
    r_tilde, D = {}, {}
    for t in months:
        om = implied_weights([S[t - i] for i in range(lookback + 1)],
                             [g[t - 1 - i] for i in range(lookback)], m)
        om_prev = implied_weights([S[t - 1 - i] for i in range(lookback + 1)],
                                  [g[t - 2 - i] for i in range(lookback)], m)
        trade = om - g[t - 1][:, None] * om_prev
        r_tilde[t] = om.T @ ret[t]
        D[t] = gamma * om.T @ sigma @ om + wealth * trade.T @ (lam[:, None] * trade)
    return r_tilde, D


def fit_aim_coefficients(r_tilde, D, months, ridge):
    """Closed-form Portfolio-ML coefficients: (mean D + ridge I)^{-1} mean r_tilde."""
    r_bar = np.mean([r_tilde[t] for t in months], axis=0)
    D_bar = np.mean([D[t] for t in months], axis=0)
    return np.linalg.solve(D_bar + ridge * np.eye(len(r_bar)), r_bar)


def objective(beta, r_tilde, D, months):
    """Average realized utility r_tilde' beta - beta' D beta / 2 over the given months."""
    return np.mean([r_tilde[t] @ beta - 0.5 * beta @ D[t] @ beta for t in months])

To see the method at work where the truth is known, the simulation below creates markets of 60 stocks over 35 years with two signals of very different persistence: a value-like signal with monthly autocorrelation 0.97 and a reversal-like signal that is independent from month to month but three times as predictive per unit. Liquidity varies across stocks by three orders of magnitude, and the investor has $10 billion and risk aversion 10. Four rules compete over the final ten years. Three of them are oracles that know the true expected returns: the cost-agnostic Markowitz portfolio, the static cost-aware portfolio, and the dynamic optimum of Equation 12.12 and Equation 12.13 computed from the known signal dynamics. Portfolio-ML knows nothing about the data-generating process and learns its coefficients from the first 25 years, once with the two signals alone and once with a liquidity rank and its interactions with the signals added as features. Repeating the exercise over 20 simulated markets guards against reading too much into one draw.

GAMMA, WEALTH, LOOKBACK = 10.0, 1e10, 11
N_STOCKS, N_MONTHS = 60, 420
TRAIN_END, TEST_START = 240, 300
RIDGES = [0.0] + list(np.exp(np.arange(-10, 7)))


def simulate_market(seed, phi_slow=0.97, b_slow=0.04, b_fast=0.12):
    """A market with one persistent and one fleeting signal and heterogeneous liquidity."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n, T = N_STOCKS, N_MONTHS
    dolvol = np.exp(rng.normal(np.log(2e8), 1.2, n))   # daily dollar volume
    lam = 0.2 / dolvol                                  # 1% of volume moves price 0.1%
    beta_mkt = rng.uniform(0.6, 1.4, n)
    ivol = rng.uniform(0.06, 0.12, n)
    sigma = 0.045**2 * np.outer(beta_mkt, beta_mkt) + np.diag(ivol**2)
    vol = np.sqrt(np.diag(sigma))

    s_slow = np.zeros((T, n))
    s_slow[0] = rng.normal(size=n)
    for t in range(1, T):
        s_slow[t] = phi_slow * s_slow[t - 1] + np.sqrt(1 - phi_slow**2) * rng.normal(size=n)
    s_fast = rng.normal(size=(T, n))

    mu = vol * (b_slow * s_slow + b_fast * s_fast)      # E_t[r_{t+1}]
    ret = mu + (np.linalg.cholesky(sigma) @ rng.normal(size=(n, T))).T
    g = (1 + ret) / (1 + ret.mean(axis=1))[:, None]     # weight growth
    liq = (np.argsort(np.argsort(dolvol)) + 0.5) / n - 0.5
    return dict(lam=lam, sigma=sigma, vol=vol, s_slow=s_slow, s_fast=s_fast,
                mu=mu, ret=ret, g=g, liq=liq, phi_slow=phi_slow,
                b_slow=b_slow, b_fast=b_fast)


def backtest(mkt, weight_rule, months):
    """Realized gross return, trading cost, and turnover of a weight rule."""
    rows, w_prev = [], np.zeros(N_STOCKS)
    for t in months:
        w_start = mkt["g"][t - 1] * w_prev
        w = weight_rule(t, w_start)
        trade = w - w_start
        rows.append({"gross": w @ mkt["ret"][t],
                     "tc": 0.5 * WEALTH * np.sum(mkt["lam"] * trade**2),
                     "turnover": np.abs(trade).sum()})
        w_prev = w
    out = pd.DataFrame(rows)
    out["net"] = out["gross"] - out["tc"]
    return out


def run_one(seed):
    mkt = simulate_market(seed)
    T, n = N_MONTHS, N_STOCKS
    sigma, lam, vol = mkt["sigma"], mkt["lam"], mkt["vol"]
    m = trading_speed(sigma, lam, WEALTH, GAMMA)
    I_n, I_m = np.eye(n), np.eye(n) - m
    sig_inv = np.linalg.inv(sigma)

    # Oracle benchmarks that know the true expected returns
    markowitz = np.einsum("ij,tj->ti", sig_inv, mkt["mu"]) / GAMMA
    static_inv = np.linalg.inv(GAMMA * sigma + WEALTH * np.diag(lam))
    mk_fast = np.einsum("ij,tj->ti", sig_inv, vol * mkt["b_fast"] * mkt["s_fast"]) / GAMMA
    mk_slow = np.einsum("ij,tj->ti", sig_inv, vol * mkt["b_slow"] * mkt["s_slow"]) / GAMMA
    slow_boost = np.linalg.solve(I_n - mkt["phi_slow"] * m, I_m)
    rules = {
        "Markowitz (oracle, cost-agnostic)": lambda t, w0: markowitz[t],
        "Static (oracle)": lambda t, w0: static_inv @ (mkt["mu"][t] + WEALTH * lam * w0),
        "Dynamic optimum (oracle)":
            lambda t, w0: m @ w0 + I_m @ (I_m @ mk_fast[t] + slow_boost @ mk_slow[t]),
    }

    # Portfolio-ML learned from data, with and without liquidity interactions
    liq = np.tile(mkt["liq"], (T, 1))
    feature_sets = {
        "Portfolio-ML: signals only": np.stack([mkt["s_slow"], mkt["s_fast"]], axis=2),
        "Portfolio-ML: signals x liquidity": np.stack(
            [mkt["s_slow"], mkt["s_fast"], liq,
             mkt["s_slow"] * liq, mkt["s_fast"] * liq], axis=2),
    }
    months_fit = range(LOOKBACK + 2, T - 1)
    train = [t for t in months_fit if t < TRAIN_END]
    valid = [t for t in months_fit if TRAIN_END <= t < TEST_START]
    for name, feats in feature_sets.items():
        S = np.stack([scale_signals(feats[t], vol) for t in range(T)])
        r_tilde, D = portfolio_ml_moments(S, mkt["g"], mkt["ret"], sigma, lam, m,
                                          WEALTH, GAMMA, months_fit, LOOKBACK)
        ridge = max(RIDGES, key=lambda l: objective(
            fit_aim_coefficients(r_tilde, D, train, l), r_tilde, D, valid))
        beta = fit_aim_coefficients(r_tilde, D, train + valid, ridge)
        rules[name] = (lambda S_, b_: lambda t, w0: m @ w0 + I_m @ (S_[t] @ b_))(S, beta)

    test = list(range(TEST_START, T - 1))
    rows = []
    for name, rule in rules.items():
        bt = backtest(mkt, rule, test)
        rows.append({"seed": seed, "Method": name,
                     "Net Sharpe": np.sqrt(12) * bt["net"].mean() / bt["net"].std(),
                     "Trading cost": 12 * bt["tc"].mean(),
                     "Utility": 12 * (bt["net"].mean() - 0.5 * GAMMA * bt["net"].var()),
                     "Turnover": bt["turnover"].mean()})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

The simulation reproduces the paper’s logic in miniature. The cost-agnostic Markowitz portfolio is destroyed by its own trading even though it knows the true expected returns. The dynamic optimum beats the static oracle in most markets although both have the same perfect one-month forecasts: aiming at today’s Markowitz portfolio ignores the persistence of the value-like signal and overpays to chase the reversal-like one. Portfolio-ML, which must learn everything from data, earns a higher net Sharpe ratio than the static oracle with roughly half its turnover, and comparable utility. It falls short of the dynamic optimum because it estimates with noise what the oracle knows.

The two Portfolio-ML variants carry a lesson about features. The optimal aim treats liquid and illiquid stocks differently: it trades toward signals quickly in liquid names and slowly in illiquid ones, and it is willing to hold persistent signals in illiquid names but not fleeting ones. A rule that is linear in the signals alone cannot express this, so it can only scale everything down, which shows up as very low turnover and lower utility. Adding liquidity and its interactions recovers much of the gap. In the paper, the random Fourier features of 115 characteristics, several of which measure liquidity, generate such interactions automatically.

def base_state(i, lookback=LOOKBACK):
    """
    Universe, covariance, and price impact at month i (no look-ahead).
    A stock is investable if it has returns over the last lookback + 2 months.
    Next month's return is used only to evaluate the decision; a missing
    value (for example, a delisting) is recorded as zero.
    """
    if i in _base_cache:
        return _base_cache[i]
    window = ret_wide.iloc[i - lookback - 1:i + 1]
    tickers = window.columns[window.notna().all()]
    cov, cov_tickers = estimate_covariance(universe_liquid, months[i])
    state = None
    if cov is not None:

        tickers = [t for t in tickers if t in cov_tickers and adv.get(t, 0) > 0]
        if len(tickers) >= 30:

    _base_cache[i] = state
    return state

def run_portfolio_ml(wealth, gamma, first_val_year=2017, first_test_year=2020,
                     p_grid=(16, 32, 64), g_grid=(np.exp(-3), np.exp(-2)),
                     ridges=(0.0, *np.exp(np.arange(-10, 7))),
                     cost_mult=1.0, long_only=False, seed=1, lookback=LOOKBACK):
    """Portfolio-ML with yearly expanding-window tuning and an out-of-sample backtest."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    states = {}
    for i in range(lookback + 2, len(months) - 1):
        st = base_state(i, lookback)
        if st is None:
            continue
        lam = cost_mult * st["lam"]
        m = trading_speed(st["sigma"], lam, wealth, gamma, mu=mkt_ret.iloc[:i].mean())
        states[i] = {**st, "lam": lam, "m": m}
    year = {i: months[i].year for i in states}
    last_year = max(year.values())

    # Step 1: objective ingredients for every (p, g) and month
    candidates = {}
    for p in p_grid:
        for g_rff in g_grid:
            W = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(g_rff), size=(len(char_cols), p // 2))
            r_tilde, D, S_now = {}, {}, {}
            for i, st in states.items():
                S_hist = month_signals(i, st["tickers"], st["vol"], W, lookback)
                om = implied_weights(S_hist[:lookback + 1],
                                     st["growth"][:lookback], st["m"])
                om_prev = implied_weights(S_hist[1:],
                                          st["growth"][1:lookback + 1], st["m"])
                trade = om - st["growth"][0][:, None] * om_prev
                r_tilde[i] = om.T @ st["ret_next"]
                D[i] = (gamma * om.T @ st["sigma"] @ om
                        + wealth * trade.T @ (st["lam"][:, None] * trade))
                S_now[i] = S_hist[0]
            # Step 2: expanding-window validation, one year at a time
            for l in ridges:
                val = {}
                for y in range(first_val_year, last_year + 1):
                    fit_m = [i for i in states if year[i] < y]
                    eval_m = [i for i in states if year[i] == y]
                    if fit_m and eval_m:
                        beta = fit_aim_coefficients(r_tilde, D, fit_m, l)
                        val[y] = objective(beta, r_tilde, D, eval_m)
                candidates[(p, g_rff, l)] = dict(r=r_tilde, D=D, S=S_now, val=val)

    # Step 3: each test year, use the best hyperparameters so far and trade
    rows, w_prev, lam_prev = [], pd.Series(dtype=float), pd.Series(dtype=float)
    for y in range(first_test_year, last_year + 1):
        best = max(candidates, key=lambda h: np.mean(
            [v for yy, v in candidates[h]["val"].items() if yy < y] or [-np.inf]))
        c = candidates[best]
        beta = fit_aim_coefficients(c["r"], c["D"],
                                    [i for i in states if year[i] < y], best[2])
        for i in [i for i in states if year[i] == y]:
            st, tick = states[i], states[i]["tickers"]
            w_start = w_prev.reindex(tick).fillna(0).values   # already grown
            aim = c["S"][i] @ beta
            w = st["m"] @ w_start + (np.eye(len(tick)) - st["m"]) @ aim
            if long_only:
                w = np.clip(w, 0, None)
            trade = w - w_start
            exits = w_prev.index.difference(tick)               # forced liquidations
            tc = (0.5 * wealth * np.sum(st["lam"] * trade**2)
                  + 0.5 * wealth * np.sum(lam_prev.reindex(exits).fillna(0).values
                                          * w_prev.reindex(exits).fillna(0).values**2))
            rows.append({"month": months[i + 1], "gross": w @ st["ret_next"], "tc": tc,
                         "turnover": np.abs(trade).sum(), "leverage": np.abs(w).sum(),
                         "p": best[0], "ridge": best[2]})
            w_prev = pd.Series(w * (1 + st["ret_next"]) / (1 + mkt_ret.iloc[i + 1]),
                               index=tick)
            lam_prev = pd.Series(st["lam"], index=tick)
    bt = pd.DataFrame(rows)
    bt["net"] = bt["gross"] - bt["tc"]
    return bt

12.10.6 What This Means for Vietnamese Research

The case for the method is stronger in Vietnam than in the US large-cap universe the authors study, because the forces it is built to handle are larger here: liquidity is concentrated in a few dozen stocks (Section 12.2), and many of the anomalies documented for Vietnamese stocks live in exactly the small, illiquid names where costs bite. Several adaptations are needed.

  1. Short selling is not available. Portfolio-ML as specified is a long-short portfolio, and in Vietnam it is a research object rather than a fund: it measures which characteristics carry value net of costs and how the frontier shrinks with size. The long-only variant in the code simply sets negative weights to zero after each rebalancing. That is a heuristic projection, not the constrained optimum. A properly constrained version maximizes the same objective numerically with \(\pi_t \geq 0\), or works with active weights around a benchmark such as VN30, where an underweight of at most the benchmark weight replaces a short position.
  2. Calibrate price impact for Vietnam and stress it. The US calibration (0.1% impact for 1% of daily volume) almost certainly understates impact for Vietnamese mid caps. Report results for cost multipliers of 1, 2, and 4 (the cost_mult argument), and compare the implied costs with the trading-cost model in Equation 12.8.
  3. Proportional costs are missing from the model. Commissions and the 0.1% selling tax are linear in the trade (Table 12.2), and linear costs create a no-trade region that a quadratic model cannot produce. Subtract them in the backtest at minimum; treat a result that survives only without them as not implementable.
  4. Respect the market’s rules. Daily price limits cap how much of a position can be traded on a given day when a stock moves sharply, and foreign ownership limits make some stocks unbuyable for foreign investors. For a foreign-investor version, drop stocks at their limits from the buy side.
  5. The sample is short. With characteristics from 2008, a 60-month covariance window, and yearly validation, only a few validation years inform the choice of hyperparameters. Keep the number of random features small, let the ridge penalty work, and report how sensitive the results are to the random seed that draws the features.
  6. Use economic feature importance as the anomaly test. Withholding one characteristic at a time and measuring the loss in net utility answers the question the factor zoo (Section 21.3) cannot: which Vietnamese anomalies are worth trading for an investor of a given size once costs are paid. The answer will differ by fund size, and the frontier across VND 100 billion, 1 trillion, and 10 trillion shows how quickly the opportunity set shrinks toward the most liquid names.

12.11 Practical Guidance for Vietnam

The preceding analysis yields several practical recommendations for researchers and investors working with Vietnamese equities:

For academic factor research: VW portfolios remain the default for asset pricing tests because they represent the investable opportunity set and avoid inflating alpha estimates with small-cap illiquidity premia. When EW portfolios are used (e.g., to give equal influence to each stock in cross-sectional sorts), researchers should report both VW and EW results and discuss the sensitivity. Fama and French (2008) follow this practice systematically.

For fund management: The choice depends on AUM and mandate. At AUM below VND 500 billion, capped VW or fundamental weighting offers a practical compromise between diversification and implementability. At larger AUM, pure VW or sector-capped VW is more realistic. Risk parity and minimum variance are suitable for low-volatility mandates but require robust covariance estimation and quarterly rebalancing.

For index construction: Vietnamese index providers (VN30, VNINDEX) use variants of capped VW. The analysis suggests that the cap level significantly affects the index’s diversification properties and tracking error relative to the uncapped VW market. A 10% cap balances concentration reduction against turnover.

For transaction cost management: In all schemes, the marginal benefit of rebalancing declines faster than the marginal cost as frequency increases beyond quarterly. Calendar-based quarterly rebalancing or threshold-based rebalancing (with a 1–2% tolerance band) provides the best cost-benefit trade-off in the Vietnamese market.

12.12 Summary

The choice of weighting scheme is not merely a technical detail—it reflects a substantive economic decision about the relative importance of diversification, investability, and cost control. In the Vietnamese market, where the capitalization distribution is highly skewed and small-cap liquidity is thin, this choice has larger consequences than in developed markets. Researchers who report results under only one weighting scheme risk conclusions that are specific to that scheme rather than reflective of a genuine economic relationship.

# Portfolio Weighting and Rebalancing

::: callout-note
In this chapter, we systematically compare portfolio weighting schemes (e.g., value-weighted, equal-weighted, and several risk-based alternativesin the Vietnamese equity market. We quantify the impact of rebalancing frequency and transaction costs on realized performance, and develop practical tools for constructing implementable portfolios under the frictions characteristic of an emerging market.
:::

Every portfolio construction decision ultimately reduces to two choices: which assets to hold, and how much to allocate to each. While earlier chapters have focused on the first question, using factor models, anomalies, and fundamental analysis to select stocks, this chapter addresses the second. The weighting scheme a researcher or investor applies can fundamentally alter the conclusions drawn from portfolio-level tests and the returns earned from an investment strategy.

The distinction matters more in Vietnam than in large, liquid markets. The Vietnamese equity market features extreme skewness in the market capitalization distribution: the top 10 firms on HOSE account for roughly 50% of total market capitalization, while hundreds of small firms contribute negligible weight. Under value-weighting, a portfolio's performance is dominated by a handful of large-cap names (Vinhomes, Vingroup, Vietcombank, FPT). Under equal-weighting, every firm contributes equally, tilting the portfolio toward small, illiquid stocks that may be expensive or impossible to trade at scale. Neither scheme is inherently correct; the choice depends on the question being asked.

This chapter develops the analytical framework for making that choice. We begin with the theoretical properties of weighting schemes, implement each scheme in practice with Vietnamese data, quantify the transaction costs of rebalancing, and extend the analysis to risk-based alternatives that explicitly incorporate the covariance structure of returns.

## Theoretical Framework {#sec-weight-theory}

### Value-Weighted Portfolios

A value-weighted (VW) portfolio allocates to each stock in proportion to its market capitalization:

$$
w_{i,t}^{VW} = \frac{\text{MCap}_{i,t}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_t} \text{MCap}_{j,t}}
$$ {#eq-vw}

where $\text{MCap}_{i,t} = P_{i,t} \times \text{Shares}_{i,t}$ is the market capitalization of stock $i$ at time $t$.

The VW portfolio has a unique theoretical status: it is the portfolio that all investors collectively hold (the "market portfolio" in CAPM). Its key properties are:

1.  **Self-rebalancing.** As prices move, weights adjust automatically. A VW portfolio requires trading only when constituents enter or leave the index, or when corporate actions (splits, issuances) change shares outstanding.
2.  **Low turnover.** Because weights drift with prices rather than being reset to targets, VW portfolios have minimal rebalancing costs.
3.  **Large-cap bias.** Returns are dominated by the largest firms. In Vietnam, this means the portfolio's risk-return profile is heavily influenced by banking, real estate, and technology conglomerates.

@hsu2004cap argues that VW portfolios are sub-optimal because they mechanically overweight overpriced stocks and underweight underpriced stocks (i.e., any deviation of price from fundamental value creates a systematic drag on VW performance relative to a fundamentally weighted alternative).

### Equal-Weighted Portfolios

An equal-weighted (EW) portfolio assigns the same weight to each constituent:

$$
w_{i,t}^{EW} = \frac{1}{N_t}
$$ {#eq-ew}

@demiguel2009optimal show that the 1/N portfolio is surprisingly competitive with mean-variance optimized portfolios, particularly when estimation windows are short and the number of assets is large (conditions that closely describe the Vietnamese market). The intuition is that estimation error in expected returns and covariances can overwhelm the gains from optimization, making the "naive" equal-weight scheme a robust default.

@plyakha2021equal decompose the EW outperformance over VW into two components:

1.  **Size tilt.** EW allocates more to small firms, which historically earn a size premium.
2.  **Rebalancing bonus.** Monthly rebalancing back to equal weights is a contrarian strategy: it sells recent winners and buys recent losers, profiting from mean reversion in individual stock returns.
<!-- 3.  **Diversification.** EW provides lower concentration and higher effective diversification. -->

However, the EW portfolio has practical disadvantages that are particularly severe in Vietnam:

-   **High turnover.** Every rebalancing date requires trading every stock back to equal weight.
-   **Illiquidity exposure.** Equal weighting of micro-cap stocks that trade VND 100 million/day alongside large-caps trading VND 500 billion/day creates severe implementation challenges.
-   **Price impact.** In a market with daily price limits ($\pm$ 7% on HOSE, $\pm$ 10% on HNX), rebalancing trades for illiquid names may hit limit-up or limit-down, preventing full execution.

### The Weighting Spectrum

Between VW and EW lies a continuum of weighting schemes. @tbl-weight-schemes summarizes the major alternatives.

| Scheme | Weight Formula | Key Property | Key Risk |
|------------------|------------------|------------------|------------------|
| Value-weighted | $w_i \propto \text{MCap}_i$ | Self-rebalancing, low turnover | Large-cap concentration |
| Equal-weighted | $w_i = 1/N$ | Maximum naive diversification | High turnover, illiquidity |
| Fundamental | $w_i \propto F_i$ (revenue, book equity, etc.) | Breaks price-value link | Requires accounting data |
| Minimum variance | $\mathbf{w} = \arg\min \mathbf{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}$ | Lowest portfolio volatility | Estimation error in $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ |
| Risk parity | $w_i \sigma_i = w_j \sigma_j \; \forall \, i,j$ | Equal risk contribution | Leverages low-vol assets |
| Maximum diversification | $\max \frac{\mathbf{w}'\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}}}$ | Maximizes diversification ratio | Sensitive to correlation estimates |
| Capped VW | $w_i \propto \text{MCap}_i$, $w_i \leq \bar{w}$ | Reduces concentration | Arbitrary cap threshold |

```{python}
def construct_universe(monthly_df, min_mcap_pct=0, min_turnover=0, min_months=12):
    """
    Construct investable universe with liquidity filters.

    Parameters
    ----------
    min_mcap_pct : float
        Exclude stocks below this market cap percentile (0-100).
    min_turnover : float
        Minimum average daily turnover in VND billion.
    min_months : int
        Minimum months of return history required.
    """
    df = monthly_df.copy()

        df["mcap_pctile"] = df.groupby("month_end")["market_cap"].transform(
            lambda x: x.rank(pct=True) * 100

    if min_turnover > 0:

    # History filter
    ticker_months = df.groupby("ticker")["month_end"].transform("count")
    df = df[ticker_months >= min_months]

    return df
```

## Implementing Weighting Schemes {#sec-weight-implementation}

We now implement each weighting scheme and compute monthly portfolio returns. All implementations follow a common structure: at each rebalancing date, compute target weights from available information, then compute the weighted return over the subsequent holding period.

### Core Portfolio Engine

```{python}
#| label: portfolio-engine
#| eval: false
#| code-summary: "Generic portfolio construction and backtesting engine"
def compute_portfolio_returns(
    monthly_df,
    weight_fn,
    rebal_freq="M",
    max_weight=1.0,
    min_weight=0.0,
):
    """
    Compute time series of portfolio returns for a given weighting function.

    Parameters
    ----------
    monthly_df : DataFrame
        Must contain 'ticker', 'month_end', 'monthly_return', and any
        columns needed by weight_fn.
    weight_fn : callable
        Function that takes a cross-section DataFrame and returns a
        Series of weights indexed by ticker. Weights need not sum to 1
        (they will be normalized).
    rebal_freq : str
        'M' for monthly, 'Q' for quarterly, 'A' for annual.
    max_weight : float
        Maximum weight per stock (for capped schemes).
    min_weight : float
        Minimum weight per stock.

    Returns
    -------
    DataFrame with columns: month_end, port_return, n_stocks,
    turnover, hhi, effective_n
    """
    months = sorted(monthly_df["month_end"].unique())

    # Determine rebalancing dates
    if rebal_freq == "M":
        rebal_dates = set(months)
    elif rebal_freq == "Q":
        rebal_dates = set(pd.to_datetime(months).to_period("Q").to_timestamp("M"))
        # Map to nearest month-end
        rebal_dates = {m for m in months if pd.Timestamp(m).month % 3 == 0}
        if not rebal_dates:
            rebal_dates = set(months[::3])
    elif rebal_freq == "A":
        rebal_dates = {m for m in months if pd.Timestamp(m).month == 6}
        if not rebal_dates:
            rebal_dates = set(months[::12])
    else:
        rebal_dates = set(months)

    results = []
    prev_weights = None

    for month in months:
        cross_section = monthly_df[monthly_df["month_end"] == month].copy()
        cross_section = cross_section.dropna(subset=["monthly_return"])

        if len(cross_section) < 5:
            continue

        if month in rebal_dates or prev_weights is None:
            # Compute fresh weights
            raw_weights = weight_fn(cross_section)
            raw_weights = raw_weights.clip(lower=min_weight, upper=max_weight)
            total = raw_weights.sum()
            if total <= 0:
                continue
            target_weights = raw_weights / total
        else:
            # Drift weights forward from previous month
            if prev_weights is not None:
                available = cross_section.set_index("ticker")
                drifted = prev_weights.reindex(available.index, fill_value=0)
                # Adjust for returns
                drifted = drifted * (1 + available["monthly_return"])
                total = drifted.sum()
                if total <= 0:
                    continue
                target_weights = drifted / total
            else:
                continue

        # Align weights with available stocks
        cross_section = cross_section.set_index("ticker")
        aligned_w = target_weights.reindex(cross_section.index, fill_value=0)
        aligned_w = aligned_w / aligned_w.sum()

        # Portfolio return
        port_ret = (aligned_w * cross_section["monthly_return"]).sum()

        # Turnover (two-way)
        if prev_weights is not None:
            prev_aligned = prev_weights.reindex(aligned_w.index, fill_value=0)
            # Drift previous weights
            prev_drifted = prev_aligned * (
                1
                + cross_section["monthly_return"].reindex(
                    prev_aligned.index, fill_value=0
                )
            )
            prev_drifted = (
                prev_drifted / prev_drifted.sum()
                if prev_drifted.sum() > 0
                else prev_drifted
            )
            turnover = (aligned_w - prev_drifted).abs().sum() / 2
        else:
            turnover = 1.0

        # Concentration metrics
        hhi = (aligned_w**2).sum()
        effective_n = 1.0 / hhi if hhi > 0 else 0

        results.append(
            {
                "month_end": month,
                "port_return": port_ret,
                "n_stocks": (aligned_w > 1e-6).sum(),
                "turnover": turnover,
                "hhi": hhi,
                "effective_n": effective_n,
            }
        )

        prev_weights = aligned_w.copy()

    return pd.DataFrame(results)
```

### Capped Value-Weighted Portfolio

To mitigate the concentration of pure VW while retaining its low-turnover properties, we impose a cap on individual stock weights. A common choice is 5% or 10%, mimicking the construction rules of capped indices such as the MSCI Capped indices.

```{python}
#| label: capped-vw
#| eval: false
#| code-summary: "Implement capped value-weighted portfolios"
def capped_vw_weights(cross_section, cap=0.05):
    """Capped VW: market cap weights with an upper bound."""
    w = cross_section.set_index('ticker')['market_cap']
    w = w / w.sum()
    # Iterative capping (redistribute excess weight)
    for _ in range(20):
        excess = w[w > cap] - cap
        if excess.sum() <= 1e-8:
            break
        w[w > cap] = cap
        w[w <= cap] *= (1 + excess.sum() / w[w <= cap].sum())
    return w
```

## Risk-Based Weighting Schemes {#sec-weight-risk-based}

The weighting schemes above use only market cap or accounting data. Risk-based schemes incorporate the covariance structure of returns, aiming to produce portfolios with better risk-adjusted performance. The trade-off is that they require estimating the covariance matrix (i.e., a high-dimensional object that is notoriously difficult to estimate precisely with short time series).

### Covariance Estimation

With $N \approx 300$ stocks and $T \approx 60$ months, the sample covariance matrix is severely ill-conditioned. We use the @ledoit2004well shrinkage estimator, which pulls the sample covariance toward a structured target (the identity matrix scaled by the average variance):

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\text{shrink}} = \delta \mathbf{F} + (1 - \delta) \mathbf{S}
$$ {#eq-shrinkage}

where $\mathbf{S}$ is the sample covariance, $\mathbf{F}$ is the shrinkage target, and $\delta \in [0,1]$ is the optimal shrinkage intensity derived analytically.

```{python}
#| label: covariance-estimation
#| eval: false
#| code-summary: "Estimate covariance matrix with Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage"
def estimate_covariance(monthly_df, month, lookback=60, min_obs=36):
    """
    Estimate covariance matrix using Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage.
    
    Parameters
    ----------
    monthly_df : DataFrame with ticker, month_end, monthly_return
    month : target month (use returns before this date)
    lookback : number of months to use
    min_obs : minimum observations per stock
    
    Returns
    -------
    cov_matrix : DataFrame (N x N)
    tickers : list of tickers with sufficient data
    """
    end_date = pd.Timestamp(month)
    start_date = end_date - pd.DateOffset(months=lookback)
    
    window = monthly_df[
        (monthly_df['month_end'] > start_date) &
        (monthly_df['month_end'] <= end_date)
    ]
    
    # Pivot to wide format
    returns_wide = window.pivot_table(
        index='month_end', columns='ticker', values='monthly_return'
    )
    
    # Keep stocks with sufficient observations
    valid_cols = returns_wide.columns[returns_wide.notna().sum() >= min_obs]
    returns_wide = returns_wide[valid_cols].dropna(axis=0, how='all')
    
    # Fill remaining NAs with 0 (conservative)
    returns_clean = returns_wide.fillna(0)
    
    if returns_clean.shape[1] < 10:
        return None, None
    
    # Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage
    lw = LedoitWolf()
    lw.fit(returns_clean.values)
    
    cov_matrix = pd.DataFrame(
        lw.covariance_,
        index=valid_cols, columns=valid_cols
    )
    
    return cov_matrix, list(valid_cols)
```

### Minimum Variance Portfolio

The global minimum variance (GMV) portfolio minimizes portfolio variance without targeting a specific return level [@clarke2011minimum]:

$$
\mathbf{w}^{MV} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}} \; \mathbf{w}'\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\mathbf{w} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{1}'\mathbf{w} = 1, \; w_i \geq 0
$$ {#eq-minvar}

The long-only constraint ($w_i \geq 0$) is essential in practice and also acts as an implicit shrinkage that improves out-of-sample performance [@jagannathan2003risk].

```{python}
#| label: minimum-variance
#| eval: false
#| code-summary: "Implement minimum variance portfolio"
def minimum_variance_weights(cov_matrix, max_weight=0.05):
    """
    Solve for the minimum variance portfolio with long-only
    and position-size constraints.
    """
    n = cov_matrix.shape[0]
    Sigma = cov_matrix.values
    
    def portfolio_variance(w):
        return w @ Sigma @ w
    
    constraints = [
        {'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda w: np.sum(w) - 1}
    ]
    bounds = [(0, max_weight) for _ in range(n)]
    x0 = np.ones(n) / n
    
    result = minimize(
        portfolio_variance, x0,
        method='SLSQP',
        bounds=bounds,
        constraints=constraints,
        options={'maxiter': 1000, 'ftol': 1e-12}
    )
    
    if result.success:
        return pd.Series(result.x, index=cov_matrix.index)
    else:
        return pd.Series(1.0 / n, index=cov_matrix.index)

def mv_weight_fn(cross_section, cov_cache={}):
    """Wrapper for portfolio engine: minimum variance."""
    month = cross_section['month_end'].iloc[0]
    
    if month not in cov_cache:
        cov_matrix, tickers = estimate_covariance(monthly, month)
        cov_cache[month] = (cov_matrix, tickers)
    
    cov_matrix, tickers = cov_cache[month]
    if cov_matrix is None:
        # Fallback to equal weight
        return pd.Series(1.0, index=cross_section.set_index('ticker').index)
    
    # Restrict to stocks in both cross-section and covariance matrix
    available = set(cross_section['ticker']) & set(tickers)
    if len(available) < 10:
        return pd.Series(1.0, index=cross_section.set_index('ticker').index)
    
    sub_cov = cov_matrix.loc[list(available), list(available)]
    weights = minimum_variance_weights(sub_cov)
    
    return weights

mv_returns = compute_portfolio_returns(
```

### Risk Parity (Equal Risk Contribution)

Risk parity allocates so that each asset contributes equally to total portfolio risk [@maillard2010properties]. The risk contribution of asset $i$ is:

$$
RC_i = w_i \cdot \frac{(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{w})_i}{\sqrt{\mathbf{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}}}
$$ {#eq-risk-contribution}

The risk parity portfolio solves $RC_i = RC_j$ for all $i, j$:

```{python}
#| label: risk-parity
#| eval: false
#| code-summary: "Implement risk parity (equal risk contribution) portfolio"
def risk_parity_weights(cov_matrix, max_weight=0.05):
    """
    Solve for the risk parity portfolio where each asset
    contributes equally to total portfolio variance.
    """
    n = cov_matrix.shape[0]
    Sigma = cov_matrix.values
    
    def risk_parity_objective(w):
        port_var = w @ Sigma @ w
        marginal = Sigma @ w
        risk_contrib = w * marginal
        target_rc = port_var / n
        return np.sum((risk_contrib - target_rc) ** 2)
    
    constraints = [
        {'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda w: np.sum(w) - 1}
    ]
    bounds = [(1e-6, max_weight) for _ in range(n)]
    x0 = np.ones(n) / n
    
    result = minimize(
        risk_parity_objective, x0,
        method='SLSQP',
        bounds=bounds,
        constraints=constraints,
        options={'maxiter': 1000, 'ftol': 1e-12}
    )
    
    if result.success:
        return pd.Series(result.x, index=cov_matrix.index)
    else:
        return pd.Series(1.0 / n, index=cov_matrix.index)

def rp_weight_fn(cross_section, cov_cache={}):
```

### Maximum Diversification Portfolio

@choueifaty2008towards define the diversification ratio as the ratio of weighted average volatility to portfolio volatility:

$$
DR(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}'\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{w}'\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{w}}}
$$ {#eq-div-ratio}

where $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ is the vector of individual asset volatilities. The maximum diversification portfolio maximizes this ratio:

```{python}
#| label: max-diversification
#| eval: false
#| code-summary: "Implement maximum diversification portfolio"
def max_diversification_weights(cov_matrix, max_weight=0.05):
    """Maximize the diversification ratio."""
    n = cov_matrix.shape[0]
    Sigma = cov_matrix.values
    sigma = np.sqrt(np.diag(Sigma))
    
    def neg_div_ratio(w):
        port_vol = np.sqrt(w @ Sigma @ w)
        if port_vol < 1e-10:
            return 0
        return -(w @ sigma) / port_vol
    
    constraints = [
        {'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda w: np.sum(w) - 1}
    ]
    bounds = [(0, max_weight) for _ in range(n)]
    x0 = np.ones(n) / n
    
    result = minimize(
        neg_div_ratio, x0,
        method='SLSQP',
        bounds=bounds,
        constraints=constraints,
        options={'maxiter': 1000, 'ftol': 1e-12}
    )
    
    if result.success:
        return pd.Series(result.x, index=cov_matrix.index)
    else:
        return pd.Series(1.0 / n, index=cov_matrix.index)
```

## Transaction Costs and Net-of-Cost Performance {#sec-weight-transaction-costs}

### Estimating Trading Costs in Vietnam

Transaction costs in Vietnam include explicit components (brokerage commissions, exchange fees, taxes) and implicit components (bid-ask spread, price impact). The explicit cost structure as of 2024 is approximately:

| Component               | Rate       | Notes                            |
|-------------------------|------------|----------------------------------|
| Brokerage commission    | 0.15–0.35% | Varies by broker and volume tier |
| Exchange & clearing fee | 0.003%     | Fixed by exchange                |
| Selling tax             | 0.10%      | Levied on gross sale proceeds    |

: Explicit transaction cost components for Vietnamese equities. {#tbl-weight-costs}

The total explicit round-trip cost (buy + sell) ranges from approximately 0.30% to 0.80%. Implicit costs—the spread and price impact—can be substantially larger for small and illiquid stocks.

We model total transaction costs as a function of trade size and stock liquidity:

$$
TC_{i,t} = c_{\text{fixed}} + \frac{1}{2} \text{Spread}_{i,t} + \lambda \sqrt{\frac{|\Delta w_{i,t}| \cdot \text{AUM}}{ADV_{i,t}}}
$$ {#eq-tc-model}

where $c_{\text{fixed}} \approx 0.25\%$ is the explicit cost per trade, $\text{Spread}_{i,t}$ is the quoted bid-ask spread, $\Delta w_{i,t}$ is the weight change, $\text{AUM}$ is portfolio size, $ADV_{i,t}$ is average daily volume in VND, and $\lambda$ is the price impact coefficient estimated from the @amihud2002illiquidity model.

```{python}
#| label: transaction-cost-model
#| eval: false
#| code-summary: "Implement transaction cost model calibrated to Vietnamese market"
def estimate_transaction_costs(weight_changes, stock_data,
                                aum_vnd=100e9, fixed_cost=0.0025,
                                impact_coef=0.10):
    """
    Estimate total transaction costs for a rebalancing event.
    
    Parameters
    ----------
    weight_changes : Series indexed by ticker, absolute weight changes
    stock_data : DataFrame with ticker, bid_ask_spread, turnover_value_avg_20d
    aum_vnd : float, portfolio AUM in VND
    fixed_cost : float, explicit cost per unit traded (one-way)
    impact_coef : float, price impact coefficient (lambda)
    
    Returns
    -------
    total_cost : float, total TC as fraction of AUM
    cost_detail : DataFrame with per-stock costs
    """
    costs = []
    
    for ticker, dw in weight_changes.items():
        if abs(dw) < 1e-6:
            continue
        
        trade_vnd = abs(dw) * aum_vnd
        
        # Explicit cost (one-way)
        explicit = fixed_cost * trade_vnd
        
        # Spread cost
        stock_info = stock_data[stock_data['ticker'] == ticker]
        if len(stock_info) > 0:
            spread = stock_info['bid_ask_spread'].iloc[0]
            adv = stock_info['turnover_value_avg_20d'].iloc[0]
        else:
            spread = 0.005  # Default 50 bps
            adv = 1e9  # Default VND 1bn
        
        spread_cost = 0.5 * spread * trade_vnd
        
        # Price impact (square root model)
        participation_rate = trade_vnd / max(adv, 1e6)
        impact_cost = impact_coef * np.sqrt(participation_rate) * trade_vnd
        
        total = explicit + spread_cost + impact_cost
        costs.append({
            'ticker': ticker,
            'weight_change': dw,
            'trade_vnd': trade_vnd,
            'explicit': explicit,
            'spread': spread_cost,
            'impact': impact_cost,
            'total': total
        })
    
    cost_df = pd.DataFrame(costs)
    total_cost = cost_df['total'].sum() / aum_vnd if len(cost_df) > 0 else 0
    
    return total_cost, cost_df
```

## Rebalancing Frequency Analysis {#sec-weight-rebalancing}

### The Rebalancing Trade-Off

Rebalancing serves two purposes: (i) restoring target weights to maintain the desired risk profile, and (ii) harvesting the "rebalancing bonus"—the systematic profit from buying low and selling high that arises when weights are reset to targets in the presence of mean-reverting cross-sectional returns.

The trade-off is clear: more frequent rebalancing maintains tighter adherence to target weights and captures more of the rebalancing bonus, but incurs higher transaction costs. The optimal frequency depends on the magnitude of mean reversion (which determines the gross rebalancing bonus), the level of transaction costs, and the rate at which weights drift from targets.

### Threshold-Based Rebalancing

An alternative to calendar-based rebalancing is to rebalance only when portfolio weights drift beyond a tolerance band. This "no-trade zone" approach is motivated by @garleanu2013dynamic, who derive the optimal dynamic trading strategy under quadratic transaction costs and show that the optimal portfolio is a weighted average of the current holdings and the frictionless target.

```{python}
#| label: threshold-rebalancing
#| eval: false
#| code-summary: "Implement threshold-based rebalancing for EW portfolio"
def compute_threshold_rebalanced(monthly_df, weight_fn,
                                  threshold=0.01):
    """
    Rebalance only when maximum weight deviation exceeds threshold.
    
    Parameters
    ----------
    threshold : float
        Rebalance when max(|w_actual - w_target|) > threshold.
    """
    months = sorted(monthly_df['month_end'].unique())
    results = []
    current_weights = None
    rebalance_count = 0
    
    for month in months:
        cs = monthly_df[monthly_df['month_end'] == month].copy()
        cs = cs.dropna(subset=['monthly_return']).set_index('ticker')
        
        if len(cs) < 5:
            continue
        
        target = weight_fn(cs.reset_index())
        target = target / target.sum()
        
        if current_weights is None:
            current_weights = target.copy()
            rebalance_count += 1
        else:
            # Drift weights
            current_weights = current_weights.reindex(cs.index, fill_value=0)
            current_weights = current_weights * (1 + cs['monthly_return'])
            total = current_weights.sum()
            if total > 0:
                current_weights = current_weights / total
            
            # Check if rebalancing needed
            max_dev = (current_weights - target.reindex(
                current_weights.index, fill_value=0
            )).abs().max()
            
            if max_dev > threshold:
                turnover = (current_weights - target.reindex(
                    current_weights.index, fill_value=0
                )).abs().sum() / 2
                current_weights = target.reindex(cs.index, fill_value=0)
                current_weights = current_weights / current_weights.sum()
                rebalance_count += 1
            else:
                turnover = 0
        
        port_ret = (current_weights.reindex(cs.index, fill_value=0) *
                    cs['monthly_return']).sum()
        hhi = (current_weights ** 2).sum()
        
        results.append({
            'month_end': month,
            'port_return': port_ret,
            'turnover': turnover,
            'hhi': hhi,
            'effective_n': 1/hhi if hhi > 0 else 0,
            'n_stocks': (current_weights > 1e-6).sum()
        })
    
    print(f"Threshold {threshold:.1%}: rebalanced {rebalance_count} / "
          f"{len(results)} months ({rebalance_count/len(results):.0%})")
    return pd.DataFrame(results)

# Test different thresholds
```

## The Rebalancing Bonus: Decomposition {#sec-weight-rebalancing-bonus}

The excess return of the rebalanced EW portfolio over a buy-and-hold EW portfolio (which starts equal-weighted but drifts) can be decomposed following @plyakha2021equal. Define $r_i$ as the return of stock $i$ over one period:

$$
R^{EW}_{\text{rebal}} - R^{EW}_{\text{drift}} \approx \frac{1}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^N \text{Var}(r_i) - \frac{1}{2N^2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\text{Cov}(r_i, r_j)
$$ {#eq-rebal-bonus}

The first term captures the "buy low, sell high" effect from resetting weights after return dispersion. The second term is the cost of undoing covariance-induced drift. The rebalancing bonus is larger when cross-sectional return dispersion is high (which it is in Vietnam) and when pairwise correlations are low.

## Machine Learning and the Implementable Efficient Frontier {#sec-weight-ief}

Every scheme in this chapter was chosen first and charged for trading afterwards. @jensen2026machine argue that this order is backwards for any strategy that trades on signals, and that the right object of evaluation is the **implementable efficient frontier**: the highest expected return *net of trading costs* attainable at each level of risk. They build a portfolio method around that objective, apply machine learning to it, and show that the ranking of strategies and of the characteristics that drive them changes once costs enter the objective rather than the accounting.

### The Problem {#sec-weight-ief-problem}

An investor with wealth $W_t$ holds portfolio weights $\pi_t$ in $N$ stocks. Between rebalancing dates the weights drift with returns, so the portfolio inherited at $t$ is $g_t \pi_{t-1}$, where $g_t$ is a diagonal matrix with entries $(1 + r^f_t + r_{i,t})/(1 + g^W_t)$ and $g^W_t$ is the growth of wealth. Moving to $\pi_t$ requires trading $W_t(\pi_t - g_t\pi_{t-1})$ in currency units, and trading moves prices linearly in the trade size, with a diagonal price-impact matrix $\Lambda_t$. The return net of the risk-free rate and of trading costs is

$$
r^{\text{net}}_{t+1} = \pi_t^\top r_{t+1} - \frac{W_t}{2}\left(\pi_t - g_t\pi_{t-1}\right)^\top \Lambda_t \left(\pi_t - g_t\pi_{t-1}\right),
$$ {#eq-weight-ief-net}

and the investor maximizes its mean minus $\gamma/2$ times its variance. Two features of @eq-weight-ief-net matter. Trading costs are quadratic in the trade, which is the linear-price-impact specification of @garleanu2013dynamic rather than the square-root model in @eq-tc-model; the choice buys tractability at the cost of understating the cost of small trades. And costs scale with wealth: the same weights cost a larger investor more. The authors calibrate impact so that trading 1% of a stock's daily dollar volume moves its price by 0.1%, which gives $\lambda_{i,t} = 0.2 / \text{ADV}_{i,t}$.

The frontier traced by varying $\gamma$ has a property the textbook frontier lacks. Without costs, any point on the capital market line is reached by levering the tangency portfolio, so the Sharpe ratio is constant along the line. With costs, larger positions mean larger trades, so the net Sharpe ratio *declines* along the implementable frontier. An investor therefore cannot maximize the net Sharpe ratio and then choose leverage; risk, return, and cost have to be chosen together. And the frontier of a larger investor lies everywhere below that of a smaller one.

### The Solution: Trade Toward an Aim {#sec-weight-ief-solution}

Generalizing @garleanu2013dynamic to stationary returns and to expected returns that are arbitrary functions of characteristics, the authors show that the optimal portfolio takes the form

$$
\pi_t = m\, g_t \pi_{t-1} + (I - m)\, A_t ,
$$ {#eq-weight-ief-rule}

so the investor starts from the inherited portfolio and trades a fraction $I - m$ of the way toward an **aim portfolio** $A_t$. The matrix $m$ solves a fixed-point equation in $\gamma\Sigma$ and $W\Lambda$ and governs trading speed: it rises toward the identity, meaning slower trading, as wealth or price impact rise and as risk aversion falls. The aim is a discounted sum of current *and expected future* Markowitz portfolios,

$$
A_t \approx (I - m)^{-1} \sum_{\tau = 0}^{\infty} (m\bar{g})^{\tau}\, c\; \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\frac{1}{\gamma}\Sigma^{-1}\mu_{t+\tau}\right],
$$ {#eq-weight-ief-aim}

with $c$ a matrix that depends on $m$. Because the investor will hold whatever it buys today for a while, a signal that predicts returns for many months is worth more than an equally strong signal that decays within a month. This is the economic content of "alpha decay," and it is exactly what a cost-agnostic or a static cost-aware portfolio ignores. The static alternative, which trades toward today's Markowitz portfolio while penalizing today's trade, has the same partial-adjustment form but aims at the wrong target.

### Portfolio-ML: Learning the Aim Directly {#sec-weight-ief-pfml}

Implementing @eq-weight-ief-aim requires forecasts of returns at every horizon. The authors' preferred method, **Portfolio-ML**, skips return forecasting altogether and learns the aim directly. It parameterizes the aim as a linear function of random Fourier features of the characteristics, scaled by each stock's volatility,

$$
A_t = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_t)^{-1}\, \text{RF}(s_t)\,\beta ,
$$ {#eq-weight-ief-parametric}

an idea that extends the parametric portfolio policies of @brandt2009parametric to a dynamic problem with trading costs. The random features, $\cos(s_{i,t}^\top w_j)$ and $\sin(s_{i,t}^\top w_j)$ for random Gaussian vectors $w_j$, let a linear model approximate nonlinear functions and interactions of the characteristics, the device whose statistical properties in return prediction are studied by @kelly2024virtue. Substituting @eq-weight-ief-parametric into @eq-weight-ief-rule makes the portfolio approximately linear in $\beta$, $\pi_t \approx \Omega_t\beta$, where $\Omega_t$ is a persistence- and growth-weighted average of current and past scaled signals. Realized utility is then quadratic in $\beta$, and the optimal coefficients have a closed form:

$$
\hat{\beta} = \left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_t D_t + \ell I\right)^{-1} \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \Omega_t^\top r_{t+1},
\qquad
D_t = \gamma\, \Omega_t^\top \Sigma_t \Omega_t + W\, \Delta\Omega_t^\top \Lambda_t\, \Delta\Omega_t ,
$$ {#eq-weight-ief-beta}

where $\Delta\Omega_t = \Omega_t - g_t\Omega_{t-1}$ is the implied trade and $\ell$ is a ridge penalty. The number of random features, their scale, and $\ell$ are chosen each year by an expanding-window validation on realized utility. No second tuning layer is needed, because the objective that fits $\beta$ is already the investor's objective.

### The Evidence {#sec-weight-ief-evidence}

The application uses US stocks above the NYSE median market capitalization, 115 characteristics from @jensen2023there, a factor-model covariance matrix in the style of commercial risk models, and an out-of-sample period from 1981 to 2020 for an investor with risk aversion 10 whose fund grows with the market to $10 billion by 2020. The figures below are from the authors' working-paper version and may differ slightly in the published article.

-   **Cost-agnostic machine learning is not implementable.** The Markowitz portfolio built on machine-learning return forecasts has a gross Sharpe ratio of 2.0 but turnover so extreme that its net return is catastrophically negative. A standard decile long-short sort on the same forecasts turns over 260% of capital a month and has a net Sharpe ratio of about −12.
-   **Static cost awareness helps but misplaces the investor.** The static method earns a positive net Sharpe ratio of 0.94 yet a negative realized utility, because its realized risk far exceeds its ex ante risk.
-   **Portfolio-ML dominates.** It earns a net Sharpe ratio of 1.38 with a monthly turnover of 32%, and the highest realized utility. Even after the authors give the forecast-based methods a second tuning layer that rescales their inputs, Portfolio-ML beats them by roughly 20% in net Sharpe ratio and 60% in utility.
-   **Costs change which characteristics matter.** Measured by the utility lost when a characteristic is withheld, the "economic feature importance" of short-term reversal, which dominates cost-agnostic models, collapses, and value and quality become the most important characteristics.

The authors' replication code is public ([GitHub: theisij/ml-and-the-implementable-efficient-frontier](https://github.com/theisij/ml-and-the-implementable-efficient-frontier)). It is written in R, requires the characteristics data of @jensen2023there from WRDS, and is designed for a computing cluster. The repository carries no license, so we do not reproduce it here. The code below is an independent Python implementation of Portfolio-ML that follows the formulas above, checked first in a simulation and then applied to Vietnamese data.

### An Independent Implementation {#sec-weight-ief-code}

The core of the method is five small functions: the trading-speed matrix $m$, the scaling of signals, random Fourier features, the implied-weights matrix $\Omega_t$, and the closed-form coefficients of @eq-weight-ief-beta.

```{python}
#| label: portfolio-ml-core
#| code-summary: "Core functions for Portfolio-ML"
def trading_speed(sigma, lam, wealth, gamma, mu=0.007, rf=0.0, iters=10):
    """
    Persistence matrix m in the optimal rule pi_t = m g_t pi_{t-1} + (I - m) A_t.

    Solves the fixed point m~ = (x + I + diag(G) - m~ o G)^{-1}, where
    x = (gamma / wealth) Lambda^{-1/2} Sigma Lambda^{-1/2}, G is the second
    moment of portfolio-weight growth, and m = Lambda^{-1/2} m~ Lambda^{1/2}.
    `lam` is the diagonal of the price-impact matrix (per unit of currency).
    """
    n = len(lam)
    G = np.ones((n, n)) + sigma / (1 + rf + mu) ** 2
    lam_isqrt = 1 / np.sqrt(lam)
    x = (gamma / wealth) * sigma * np.outer(lam_isqrt, lam_isqrt)
    # Start from the closed-form solution when weight growth is deterministic
    ev, evec = np.linalg.eigh(x + 2 * np.eye(n))
    m_tilde = evec @ np.diag(0.5 * (ev - np.sqrt(ev**2 - 4))) @ evec.T
    base = x + np.eye(n) + np.diag(np.diag(G))
    for _ in range(iters):
        m_tilde = np.linalg.inv(base - m_tilde * G)
    return lam_isqrt[:, None] * m_tilde * np.sqrt(lam)[None, :]


def scale_signals(features, vol):
    """Demean features, add a constant, give each column unit length, divide rows by volatility."""
    f = features - features.mean(axis=0)
    f = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(f)), f])
    f = f / np.sqrt((f**2).sum(axis=0))
    return f / vol[:, None]


def random_fourier_features(features, W):
    """Random Fourier features: cos and sin of random projections of the features."""
    z = features @ W
    return np.hstack([np.cos(z), np.sin(z)])


def implied_weights(S_hist, g_hist, m):
    """
    Omega_t such that the portfolio implied by aim coefficients beta is Omega_t beta.
    S_hist[i] is the scaled signal matrix i months ago (i = 0 is today);
    g_hist[i] is the weight-growth vector realized over the month ending i months ago.
    """
    n = m.shape[0]
    M, num, den = np.eye(n), np.zeros_like(S_hist[0]), np.zeros((n, n))
    for i, S_i in enumerate(S_hist):
        num += M @ S_i
        den += M
        if i < len(g_hist):
            M = M @ m @ np.diag(g_hist[i])
    return np.linalg.solve(den, num)


def portfolio_ml_moments(S, g, ret, sigma, lam, m, wealth, gamma, months, lookback=11):
    """Per-month ingredients of the Portfolio-ML objective: r_tilde_t and D_t."""
    r_tilde, D = {}, {}
    for t in months:
        om = implied_weights([S[t - i] for i in range(lookback + 1)],
                             [g[t - 1 - i] for i in range(lookback)], m)
        om_prev = implied_weights([S[t - 1 - i] for i in range(lookback + 1)],
                                  [g[t - 2 - i] for i in range(lookback)], m)
        trade = om - g[t - 1][:, None] * om_prev
        r_tilde[t] = om.T @ ret[t]
        D[t] = gamma * om.T @ sigma @ om + wealth * trade.T @ (lam[:, None] * trade)
    return r_tilde, D


def fit_aim_coefficients(r_tilde, D, months, ridge):
    """Closed-form Portfolio-ML coefficients: (mean D + ridge I)^{-1} mean r_tilde."""
    r_bar = np.mean([r_tilde[t] for t in months], axis=0)
    D_bar = np.mean([D[t] for t in months], axis=0)
    return np.linalg.solve(D_bar + ridge * np.eye(len(r_bar)), r_bar)


def objective(beta, r_tilde, D, months):
    """Average realized utility r_tilde' beta - beta' D beta / 2 over the given months."""
    return np.mean([r_tilde[t] @ beta - 0.5 * beta @ D[t] @ beta for t in months])
```

To see the method at work where the truth is known, the simulation below creates markets of 60 stocks over 35 years with two signals of very different persistence: a value-like signal with monthly autocorrelation 0.97 and a reversal-like signal that is independent from month to month but three times as predictive per unit. Liquidity varies across stocks by three orders of magnitude, and the investor has $10 billion and risk aversion 10. Four rules compete over the final ten years. Three of them are *oracles* that know the true expected returns: the cost-agnostic Markowitz portfolio, the static cost-aware portfolio, and the dynamic optimum of @eq-weight-ief-rule and @eq-weight-ief-aim computed from the known signal dynamics. Portfolio-ML knows nothing about the data-generating process and learns its coefficients from the first 25 years, once with the two signals alone and once with a liquidity rank and its interactions with the signals added as features. Repeating the exercise over 20 simulated markets guards against reading too much into one draw.

```{python}
def simulate_market(seed, phi_slow=0.97, b_slow=0.04, b_fast=0.12):
    """A market with one persistent and one fleeting signal and heterogeneous liquidity."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    n, T = N_STOCKS, N_MONTHS
    dolvol = np.exp(rng.normal(np.log(2e8), 1.2, n))   # daily dollar volume
    lam = 0.2 / dolvol                                  # 1% of volume moves price 0.1%
    beta_mkt = rng.uniform(0.6, 1.4, n)
    ivol = rng.uniform(0.06, 0.12, n)
    sigma = 0.045**2 * np.outer(beta_mkt, beta_mkt) + np.diag(ivol**2)
    vol = np.sqrt(np.diag(sigma))

    s_slow = np.zeros((T, n))
    s_slow[0] = rng.normal(size=n)
    for t in range(1, T):
        s_slow[t] = phi_slow * s_slow[t - 1] + np.sqrt(1 - phi_slow**2) * rng.normal(size=n)
    s_fast = rng.normal(size=(T, n))

    mu = vol * (b_slow * s_slow + b_fast * s_fast)      # E_t[r_{t+1}]
    ret = mu + (np.linalg.cholesky(sigma) @ rng.normal(size=(n, T))).T
    g = (1 + ret) / (1 + ret.mean(axis=1))[:, None]     # weight growth
    liq = (np.argsort(np.argsort(dolvol)) + 0.5) / n - 0.5
    return dict(lam=lam, sigma=sigma, vol=vol, s_slow=s_slow, s_fast=s_fast,
                mu=mu, ret=ret, g=g, liq=liq, phi_slow=phi_slow,
                b_slow=b_slow, b_fast=b_fast)

        trade = w - w_start
        rows.append({"gross": w @ mkt["ret"][t],
                     "tc": 0.5 * WEALTH * np.sum(mkt["lam"] * trade**2),
                     "turnover": np.abs(trade).sum()})
        w_prev = w
    out = pd.DataFrame(rows)
    out["net"] = out["gross"] - out["tc"]
    return out


def run_one(seed):
    mkt = simulate_market(seed)
    T, n = N_MONTHS, N_STOCKS
    sigma, lam, vol = mkt["sigma"], mkt["lam"], mkt["vol"]
    m = trading_speed(sigma, lam, WEALTH, GAMMA)
    I_n, I_m = np.eye(n), np.eye(n) - m
    sig_inv = np.linalg.inv(sigma)

    # Oracle benchmarks that know the true expected returns
    markowitz = np.einsum("ij,tj->ti", sig_inv, mkt["mu"]) / GAMMA
    static_inv = np.linalg.inv(GAMMA * sigma + WEALTH * np.diag(lam))
    mk_fast = np.einsum("ij,tj->ti", sig_inv, vol * mkt["b_fast"] * mkt["s_fast"]) / GAMMA
    mk_slow = np.einsum("ij,tj->ti", sig_inv, vol * mkt["b_slow"] * mkt["s_slow"]) / GAMMA
    slow_boost = np.linalg.solve(I_n - mkt["phi_slow"] * m, I_m)
    rules = {
        "Markowitz (oracle, cost-agnostic)": lambda t, w0: markowitz[t],
        "Static (oracle)": lambda t, w0: static_inv @ (mkt["mu"][t] + WEALTH * lam * w0),
        "Dynamic optimum (oracle)":
            lambda t, w0: m @ w0 + I_m @ (I_m @ mk_fast[t] + slow_boost @ mk_slow[t]),
    }

    # Portfolio-ML learned from data, with and without liquidity interactions
    liq = np.tile(mkt["liq"], (T, 1))
    feature_sets = {
        "Portfolio-ML: signals only": np.stack([mkt["s_slow"], mkt["s_fast"]], axis=2),
        "Portfolio-ML: signals x liquidity": np.stack(
            [mkt["s_slow"], mkt["s_fast"], liq,
             mkt["s_slow"] * liq, mkt["s_fast"] * liq], axis=2),
    }
    months_fit = range(LOOKBACK + 2, T - 1)
    train = [t for t in months_fit if t < TRAIN_END]
    valid = [t for t in months_fit if TRAIN_END <= t < TEST_START]
    for name, feats in feature_sets.items():
        S = np.stack([scale_signals(feats[t], vol) for t in range(T)])
        r_tilde, D = portfolio_ml_moments(S, mkt["g"], mkt["ret"], sigma, lam, m,
                                          WEALTH, GAMMA, months_fit, LOOKBACK)
        ridge = max(RIDGES, key=lambda l: objective(
            fit_aim_coefficients(r_tilde, D, train, l), r_tilde, D, valid))
        beta = fit_aim_coefficients(r_tilde, D, train + valid, ridge)
        rules[name] = (lambda S_, b_: lambda t, w0: m @ w0 + I_m @ (S_[t] @ b_))(S, beta)

    test = list(range(TEST_START, T - 1))
    rows = []
    for name, rule in rules.items():
        bt = backtest(mkt, rule, test)
        rows.append({"seed": seed, "Method": name,
                     "Net Sharpe": np.sqrt(12) * bt["net"].mean() / bt["net"].std(),
                     "Trading cost": 12 * bt["tc"].mean(),
                     "Utility": 12 * (bt["net"].mean() - 0.5 * GAMMA * bt["net"].var()),
                     "Turnover": bt["turnover"].mean()})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)


sim_results = pd.concat([run_one(seed) for seed in range(20)],
                        ignore_index=True)
```

The simulation reproduces the paper's logic in miniature. The cost-agnostic Markowitz portfolio is destroyed by its own trading even though it knows the true expected returns. The dynamic optimum beats the static oracle in most markets although both have the same perfect one-month forecasts: aiming at today's Markowitz portfolio ignores the persistence of the value-like signal and overpays to chase the reversal-like one. Portfolio-ML, which must learn everything from data, earns a higher net Sharpe ratio than the static oracle with roughly half its turnover, and comparable utility. It falls short of the dynamic optimum because it estimates with noise what the oracle knows.

The two Portfolio-ML variants carry a lesson about features. The optimal aim treats liquid and illiquid stocks differently: it trades toward signals quickly in liquid names and slowly in illiquid ones, and it is willing to hold persistent signals in illiquid names but not fleeting ones. A rule that is linear in the signals alone cannot express this, so it can only scale everything down, which shows up as very low turnover and lower utility. Adding liquidity and its interactions recovers much of the gap. In the paper, the random Fourier features of 115 characteristics, several of which measure liquidity, generate such interactions automatically.

Applying the method to Vietnamese stocks uses the liquid universe and the Ledoit-Wolf covariance estimator defined earlier in this chapter, the characteristics panel from the factor-construction chapter, and price impact calibrated to each stock's average daily traded value in VND. The code is not executed here: the hyperparameter search refits the model for every combination of random features, feature scale, and ridge penalty, and takes several minutes per wealth and risk-aversion pair.

```{python}
def base_state(i, lookback=LOOKBACK):
    """
    Universe, covariance, and price impact at month i (no look-ahead).
    A stock is investable if it has returns over the last lookback + 2 months.
    Next month's return is used only to evaluate the decision; a missing
    value (for example, a delisting) is recorded as zero.
    """
    if i in _base_cache:
        return _base_cache[i]
    window = ret_wide.iloc[i - lookback - 1:i + 1]
    tickers = window.columns[window.notna().all()]
    cov, cov_tickers = estimate_covariance(universe_liquid, months[i])
    state = None
    if cov is not None:
        adv = adv_wide.iloc[i]
        tickers = [t for t in tickers if t in cov_tickers and adv.get(t, 0) > 0]
        if len(tickers) >= 30:

                         ret_next=ret_wide.iloc[i + 1][tickers].fillna(0).values)
    _base_cache[i] = state
    return state
```

```{python}
#| code-summary: "Tuning and out-of-sample backtest of Portfolio-ML"
def run_portfolio_ml(wealth, gamma, first_val_year=2017, first_test_year=2020,
                     p_grid=(16, 32, 64), g_grid=(np.exp(-3), np.exp(-2)),
                     ridges=(0.0, *np.exp(np.arange(-10, 7))),
                     cost_mult=1.0, long_only=False, seed=1, lookback=LOOKBACK):
    """Portfolio-ML with yearly expanding-window tuning and an out-of-sample backtest."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    states = {}
    for i in range(lookback + 2, len(months) - 1):
        st = base_state(i, lookback)
        if st is None:
            continue
        lam = cost_mult * st["lam"]
        m = trading_speed(st["sigma"], lam, wealth, gamma, mu=mkt_ret.iloc[:i].mean())
        states[i] = {**st, "lam": lam, "m": m}
    year = {i: months[i].year for i in states}
    last_year = max(year.values())

    # Step 1: objective ingredients for every (p, g) and month
    candidates = {}
    for p in p_grid:
        for g_rff in g_grid:
            W = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(g_rff), size=(len(char_cols), p // 2))
            r_tilde, D, S_now = {}, {}, {}
            for i, st in states.items():
                S_hist = month_signals(i, st["tickers"], st["vol"], W, lookback)
                om = implied_weights(S_hist[:lookback + 1],
                                     st["growth"][:lookback], st["m"])
                om_prev = implied_weights(S_hist[1:],
                                          st["growth"][1:lookback + 1], st["m"])
                trade = om - st["growth"][0][:, None] * om_prev
                r_tilde[i] = om.T @ st["ret_next"]
                D[i] = (gamma * om.T @ st["sigma"] @ om
                        + wealth * trade.T @ (st["lam"][:, None] * trade))
                S_now[i] = S_hist[0]
            # Step 2: expanding-window validation, one year at a time
            for l in ridges:
                val = {}
                for y in range(first_val_year, last_year + 1):
                    fit_m = [i for i in states if year[i] < y]
                    eval_m = [i for i in states if year[i] == y]
                    if fit_m and eval_m:
                        beta = fit_aim_coefficients(r_tilde, D, fit_m, l)
                        val[y] = objective(beta, r_tilde, D, eval_m)
                candidates[(p, g_rff, l)] = dict(r=r_tilde, D=D, S=S_now, val=val)

    # Step 3: each test year, use the best hyperparameters so far and trade
    rows, w_prev, lam_prev = [], pd.Series(dtype=float), pd.Series(dtype=float)
    for y in range(first_test_year, last_year + 1):
        best = max(candidates, key=lambda h: np.mean(
            [v for yy, v in candidates[h]["val"].items() if yy < y] or [-np.inf]))
        c = candidates[best]
        beta = fit_aim_coefficients(c["r"], c["D"],
                                    [i for i in states if year[i] < y], best[2])
        for i in [i for i in states if year[i] == y]:
            st, tick = states[i], states[i]["tickers"]
            w_start = w_prev.reindex(tick).fillna(0).values   # already grown
            aim = c["S"][i] @ beta
            w = st["m"] @ w_start + (np.eye(len(tick)) - st["m"]) @ aim
            if long_only:
                w = np.clip(w, 0, None)
            trade = w - w_start
            exits = w_prev.index.difference(tick)               # forced liquidations
            tc = (0.5 * wealth * np.sum(st["lam"] * trade**2)
                  + 0.5 * wealth * np.sum(lam_prev.reindex(exits).fillna(0).values
                                          * w_prev.reindex(exits).fillna(0).values**2))
            rows.append({"month": months[i + 1], "gross": w @ st["ret_next"], "tc": tc,
                         "turnover": np.abs(trade).sum(), "leverage": np.abs(w).sum(),
                         "p": best[0], "ridge": best[2]})
            w_prev = pd.Series(w * (1 + st["ret_next"]) / (1 + mkt_ret.iloc[i + 1]),
                               index=tick)
            lam_prev = pd.Series(st["lam"], index=tick)
    bt = pd.DataFrame(rows)
    bt["net"] = bt["gross"] - bt["tc"]
    return bt

    return pd.Series({

        "Net Sharpe": np.sqrt(12) * bt["net"].mean() / bt["net"].std(),
        "Utility": 12 * (bt["net"].mean() - 0.5 * gamma * bt["net"].var()),
        "Turnover": bt["turnover"].mean(),
        "Leverage": bt["leverage"].mean(),
    })
```

### What This Means for Vietnamese Research {#sec-weight-ief-vietnam}

The case for the method is stronger in Vietnam than in the US large-cap universe the authors study, because the forces it is built to handle are larger here: liquidity is concentrated in a few dozen stocks (@sec-weight-data), and many of the anomalies documented for Vietnamese stocks live in exactly the small, illiquid names where costs bite. Several adaptations are needed.

1.  **Short selling is not available.** Portfolio-ML as specified is a long-short portfolio, and in Vietnam it is a research object rather than a fund: it measures which characteristics carry value net of costs and how the frontier shrinks with size. The long-only variant in the code simply sets negative weights to zero after each rebalancing. That is a heuristic projection, not the constrained optimum. A properly constrained version maximizes the same objective numerically with $\pi_t \geq 0$, or works with active weights around a benchmark such as VN30, where an underweight of at most the benchmark weight replaces a short position.
2.  **Calibrate price impact for Vietnam and stress it.** The US calibration (0.1% impact for 1% of daily volume) almost certainly understates impact for Vietnamese mid caps. Report results for cost multipliers of 1, 2, and 4 (the `cost_mult` argument), and compare the implied costs with the trading-cost model in @eq-tc-model.
3.  **Proportional costs are missing from the model.** Commissions and the 0.1% selling tax are linear in the trade (@tbl-weight-costs), and linear costs create a no-trade region that a quadratic model cannot produce. Subtract them in the backtest at minimum; treat a result that survives only without them as not implementable.
4.  **Respect the market's rules.** Daily price limits cap how much of a position can be traded on a given day when a stock moves sharply, and foreign ownership limits make some stocks unbuyable for foreign investors. For a foreign-investor version, drop stocks at their limits from the buy side.
5.  **The sample is short.** With characteristics from 2008, a 60-month covariance window, and yearly validation, only a few validation years inform the choice of hyperparameters. Keep the number of random features small, let the ridge penalty work, and report how sensitive the results are to the random seed that draws the features.
6.  **Use economic feature importance as the anomaly test.** Withholding one characteristic at a time and measuring the loss in net utility answers the question the factor zoo (@sec-factor-zoo-multiple-testing) cannot: which Vietnamese anomalies are worth trading for an investor of a given size once costs are paid. The answer will differ by fund size, and the frontier across VND 100 billion, 1 trillion, and 10 trillion shows how quickly the opportunity set shrinks toward the most liquid names.

## Practical Guidance for Vietnam {#sec-weight-practical}

The preceding analysis yields several practical recommendations for researchers and investors working with Vietnamese equities:

**For academic factor research:** VW portfolios remain the default for asset pricing tests because they represent the investable opportunity set and avoid inflating alpha estimates with small-cap illiquidity premia. When EW portfolios are used (e.g., to give equal influence to each stock in cross-sectional sorts), researchers should report both VW and EW results and discuss the sensitivity. @fama2008dissecting follow this practice systematically.

**For fund management:** The choice depends on AUM and mandate. At AUM below VND 500 billion, capped VW or fundamental weighting offers a practical compromise between diversification and implementability. At larger AUM, pure VW or sector-capped VW is more realistic. Risk parity and minimum variance are suitable for low-volatility mandates but require robust covariance estimation and quarterly rebalancing.

**For index construction:** Vietnamese index providers (VN30, VNINDEX) use variants of capped VW. The analysis suggests that the cap level significantly affects the index's diversification properties and tracking error relative to the uncapped VW market. A 10% cap balances concentration reduction against turnover.

**For transaction cost management:** In all schemes, the marginal benefit of rebalancing declines faster than the marginal cost as frequency increases beyond quarterly. Calendar-based quarterly rebalancing or threshold-based rebalancing (with a 1–2% tolerance band) provides the best cost-benefit trade-off in the Vietnamese market.
